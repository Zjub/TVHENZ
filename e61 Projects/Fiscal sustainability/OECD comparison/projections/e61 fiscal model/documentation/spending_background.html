Constructing Australian government spending

e61 fiscal model | Discussion paper | 28 October 2026

This paper documents a consolidated-government scenario framework. Sources support the identified inputs, not every parameter choice. Sourced amounts, calibrated allocations, scope proxies, modelling judgments and accounting identities are distinguished.

Starting data and calibration

PBO national primary expenses provide the common FY2029-30 anchor of $1,171.9bn. ABS GFS functional controls, AIHW allocations, NDIA plan expenses and other programme sources allocate it across eight functions and 24 subfunctions. Health categories follow AIHW service areas, combining Commonwealth and state/territory government funding and reconciling to the ABS COFOG/GFS health total. They differ from the IGR Commonwealth programme categories. IGR age-cost profiles are used as demographic proxies; using those profiles does not make the spending categories equivalent. The 2025-2030 bridge is an allocation calculation; post-2030 scenario controls do not recalibrate it. Internal transfers are not added as extra national spending.

Annual demographics

All functions share the official 2026 IGR annual population projection by single-year age and sex for 2026-2066, with Population Statement 2025 supplying the 2025 base year. Health and aged care apply their fixed published age-cost weights to these annual cells; education uses its age-window proxies; NDIS and Age Pension follow annual cohorts. Residual population-based demand remains a total-population proxy. The population-growth slider scales every age/sex cell equally within each year, retaining the official projected age distribution and leaving migration, fertility and mortality assumptions unchanged.

A consistent identity

Spending = anchor x population x age/cohort demand x coverage x general prices x real unit cost x additional service growth. Defence and pension payments have explicit policy rules.

Real unit cost = (1 - L) + L x W / Q. L is the fixed labour share, W the real wage index and Q the service-productivity index. Q = (1 + theta x economy-wide productivity growth)^years. Real wages follow economy-wide productivity. Efficiency reduces labour requirements, while non-labour inputs follow general inflation.

A wage-productivity gap generates Baumol cost pressure inside the input basket. Additional service growth, (1 + r)^years, represents intensity, quality and mix beyond that basket. It is not an additional Baumol premium. Funding-index, CPI and cash-indexation proxies use their stated rules rather than resource-efficiency deductions.

Health and education efficiency defaults to 25% of economy-wide productivity (0.3% annually at the macro default). Health adds real-GDP-per-person demand with income elasticity 0.8 and defaults to zero remaining pressures beyond explicit income and costs. Education retains 0.5% additional annual cost growth. Both are arbitrary discussion assumptions, not empirical estimates. The controls display actual additional-growth rates and preserve subfunction differences by applying a common shift. NDIS and cash transfers receive no service-efficiency deduction. Aged care retains 0.3% additional growth and other public services zero, with no new efficiency default.

Cost assumption sets

Current assumptions remain the reference. Health historical options use income-adjusted matched 2016-2025 residuals or a 1999-2025 aggregate consumption residual. Education retains its 2016-2025 option. See health_framework_background.pdf for equations, price conventions and the long-history accounting bridge. Aggregate adjustments add percentage points to each subfunction rate. Estimates bundle policy, coverage, quality and mix, and do not identify separate efficiency effects. Higher education and VET share a tertiary estimate. The reconciliation block is a heterogeneous accounting residual. The Spending tab compares both sets with selected economic and policy settings held fixed.

NDIS uses the existing participant/cohort projection, original plan-cost index and 0.5% additional cost/use growth, plus its policy adjustment. Other transfers use the original demand and payment-indexation proxy. Age Pension retains means tests and wealth accumulation. Transfers have no service-efficiency deductions. These controls are presented separately from health and education efficiency and cost growth.

IGR and literature discipline

Commonwealth IGR Appendix A3 describes programme-specific real age/sex-adjusted health trends; numeric coefficients are not supplied in the reviewed appendix. NDIS costs by age/disability transition to earnings growth. Commonwealth schools use 75% wages/25% CPI and university grants use CPI. VET uses a wage-cost index; the numerical 69.75% comparator here is the National Skills Agreement proxy.

Comparators and reference implied growth are fixed at the reference macro assumptions. They are not targets for differently defined consolidated expenditure. State resource costs and Commonwealth funding rules need not match. A blank coefficient is reported as unavailable rather than invented.

What the decomposition measures

Exact Shapley accounting allocates interactions across income-related demand, population, age/cohort demand, coverage, general prices, real wages/funding indexation, service productivity, additional service growth, policy/financing and the GDP denominator. Contributions reconcile before rounding. They describe arithmetic, not estimated causal effects. Price growth is removed for 2030-dollar results; the denominator is included only for GDP shares.

Other functions and fiscal financing

Aged care uses an IGR demand profile with four allocations. Other public services use population demand. Other social protection is a residual cash/indexation proxy, not a complete benefit microsimulation. Pension receipt and payments follow public-data calibrated wealth cohorts and means tests; joint distributions and transitions need future HILDA estimation; defence follows a stated GDP-share target. Gross debt finances deficits and asset acquisitions. Interest is charged on opening gross debt, with separate asset receipts.

Fixed-share policies and borrowing-rate sensitivity

Each function can optionally be fixed at a selected GDP share from 2031, preserving the common 2030 anchor. The target replaces the function total and is allocated across its subfunctions proportionately to unconstrained spending. Annual exports show unconstrained costs, actual costs and the policy factor. The override feeds into the fiscal balance and debt; a zero target is supported.

The borrowing-rate sensitivity defaults to zero. A setting of 3 basis points per percentage point of net debt/GDP matches the historical Treasury research benchmark in Yan and Brittle (2010). The rate adjustment is sensitivity/10000 times the previous-year net-debt/GDP percentage-point gap from 2030. It can be negative when debt falls, with a zero borrowing-rate floor. Interest is charged on opening gross debt; asset yields do not change. Applying a long-run real ten-year yield-margin estimate immediately to the effective rate is a scenario assumption, without refinancing lags.

Discussion questions

What quality-adjusted service-productivity response is credible? How much additional growth remains after explicit costs? Do enrolment, morbidity and participant-mix changes warrant separate demand assumptions? Which funding rules require their own blocks? What data and uncertainty range support each proposal?
