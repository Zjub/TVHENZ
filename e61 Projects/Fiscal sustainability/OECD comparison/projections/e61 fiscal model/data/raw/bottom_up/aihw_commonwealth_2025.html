Government sources: Australian government spending

On this page In this section

Australian Government spending Spending relative to government expenses Spending programs Area of spending Private health insurance premium rebates Department of Veterans’ Affairs spending Department of Defence health spending

Australian Government spending

In 2024–25, Australian Government health spending was $113.9 billion, an increase of $2.6 billion (2.3%) in real terms from 2023–24 (Table 10). This was the first increase since 2021–22, reversing the declines in 2022–23 and 2023–24, although growth remained below the average annual real growth rate of 2.4% over the decade to 2024–25.

The increase was driven by higher funding to states and territories (3.4%) and direct Australian Government health spending (1.9%). Spending on the private health insurance rebate rose by 1.0% (Table 12). The largest increases were in public hospital services, referred medical services, health and medical research, and community health.

Spending relative to government expenses

In 2024–25, the Australian Government spent $113.9 billion on health, representing 15.3% of total government expenses, down from 15.5% in 2023–24 (Figure 9). Although Australian Government health spending increased by 7.2% in nominal terms, total government expenses grew more strongly at 8.4%, resulting in a smaller share of government spending being allocated to health (Table 11).

Figure 9: Ratio of Australian Government health spending to Australian Government expenses, current prices, 2014–15 to 2024–25

The line graph shows the dollar amounts of Australian Government expenses and health spending, along with an additional line representing the ratio of health spending to total government expenses as a percentage. Australian Government health spending increased from $66.2 billion in 2014–15 to $113.9 billion in 2024–25. Government expenses rose from $419.0 billion in 2014–15 to $745.0 billion in 2024–25. The highest ratio of health spending to government expenses was 17.0% in 2021–22, while the lowest was 14.3% in 2020–21.

Sources: AIHW Health Expenditure Database; Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS 2026c) (Table 11).

Spending programs

Australian Government spending in 2024–25 (Figure 10) comprised:

  • direct Australian Government spending ($69.5 billion, or 61.1%), mostly administered through the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing on programs for which the government has responsibility, such as the MBS, PBS, and health research. This also includes some health spending by Defence ($779 million).
  • funding to states and territories ($33.6 billion, or 29.5%), including National Health Reform funding (mainly for public hospital services), other schedules to the Federation Funding Agreements – Health and the PBS Section 100 funding for public hospital services.
  • rebates and subsidies for privately insured individuals under the national Private Health Insurance Act 2007 ($7.2 billion, or 6.3%).
  • DVA funding for goods and services provided to eligible veterans and their dependants ($3.5 billion, or 3.1%).

The 2.3% increase in Australian Government health spending between 2023–24 and 2024–25 can be attributed to increased expenditure through specific Department of Health, Disability and Ageing programs ($1.3 billion increase) and funding to states and territories through grants ($1.1billion). The main driver of these increases was the increased investment in health service delivery and associated program funding.

Figure 10: Australian Government total health spending by program, constant prices (a), 2014–15 to 2024–25

The line graph shows Australian Government spending from 2014–15 to 2024–25 across four categories: Own program spending, Grants to states, Health insurance premium rebates, and the Department of Veterans’ Affairs. From 2014–15 to 2021–22, health spending generally increased across all categories except for the Department of Veterans’ Affairs and health insurance premium rebates, which showed little or no growth. In 2024–25, the Australian Government spent $69.5 billion on its own programs, $33.6 billion on grants to states, $7.2 billion on health insurance premium rebates, and $3.5 billion on the Department of Veterans’ Affairs.

MBS, PBS and RPBS government benefits paid in 2024–25

In 2024–25, the Australian Government funded $32.1 billion as government benefits paid for MBS services.

During the same year, the Australian Government funded $18.6 billion as subsidies for PBS and $0.4 billion for RPBS pharmaceuticals.

Area of spending

During 2024–25, more than one-third (34.8%) of Australian Government health spending was allocated to primary health care including public health, totalling $39.7 billion – a real decrease of 1.3% from 2023–24 (Figure 11). Of this:

  • pharmaceuticals subsidised through the PBS (not including Section 100 drugs and other drugs that could be allocated to the areas of public hospital services and private hospitals) contributed $15.8 billion.
  • unreferred medical services (mainly visits to a general practitioner) was $12.2 billion.
  • public health was $3.6 billion.
  • other health practitioner services were $3.4 billion (Table A6).

Figure 11: Australian Government health spending, by area of spending, constant prices (a), 2014–15 to 2024–25

The line graph shows Australian Government health spending across key areas of spending from 2014–15 to 2024–25, including public hospitals, primary health care, referred medical services, research, private hospitals, and other services. From 2014–15 to 2024–25, spending increased across all categories. In 2024–25, the Australian Government spent $39.7 billion on primary health care, $35.4 billion on public hospital services, $18.1 billion on referred medical services, $8.3 billion on private hospitals, $6.8 billion on research, and $5.6 billion on other services.

Notes: 

  1. Other services include patient transport services, aids and appliances, and administration.
  2. Spending on the medical expenses tax rebate and capital is not included.

Public hospital services were the second largest component of Australian Government health spending in 2024–25, at $35.4 billion (a real increase of 3.1% from 2023–24), followed by referred medical services at $18.1 billion (a real increase of 4.6%) (Figure 11, Table 13). Australian Government spending on public hospital services includes an estimated $912 million in Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS) benefits for in-hospital services, based on Hospital Casemix Protocol (HCP) data. This estimate is unlikely to capture the full amount of MBS spending on non-admitted patients, which the National Health Funding Body (NHFB) estimates could be as high as $1,083 million. The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) is working with the Department, NHFB and the Health Expenditure Advisory Committee (HEAC) to address this data gap.

The amount of $35.4 billion of the Australian Government spending on public hospital services includes:

  • National Health Reform funding – the Australian Government funding for public hospital services under NHRA
  • PBS section 100 programs (Highly Specialised Drugs, PBS Efficient Funding of Chemotherapy program, Chemotherapy Pharmaceutical Access Program (CPAP) and the Special Authority Program (trastuzumab - Herceptin), Botulinum Toxin Program, and Human Growth Hormone program) delivered through hospitals
  • some specific programs administered by the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing
  • In-hospital MBS services
  • Other schedules to the Federation Funding Agreement (FFA) – Health
  • Health spending by DVA
  • Premium rebates (an allocation of the private health premium rebates)
  • Department of Defence and capital consumption allocated to public hospital services. More details can be found in Table A11.

The growth in total Australian Government spending between 2023–24 and 2024–25 was driven mainly by higher spending on public hospital services ($1.1 billion), referred medical services ($0.8 billion), and health research ($0.7 billion) (Figure 11).

Over the decade since 2014–15, private hospitals experienced the highest average annual growth rate in the Australian Government health spending (4.5% per year), followed by public hospital services (3.7% per year) and primary health care including public health (2.0% per year) (Figure 11).

Private health insurance premium rebates

In 2024–25, the Australian Government’s private health insurance premium rebate was $7.2 billion, an increase of $74 million (1.0% in real terms) from 2023–24 (Figure 12). This estimate includes only rebates paid as health benefits and excludes non-health costs such as insurance advertising. Consequently, it is lower than the total rebate paid to reduce premiums reported elsewhere, including Department of Health, Disability and Ageing and Australian Taxation Office annual reports.

More details on the estimation can be found in the Overview of data sources and methodology.

The line graph shows that health insurance premium rebates fluctuated around $7.0 billion to $7.4 billion between 2014–15 and 2024–25.

Department of Veterans’ Affairs spending

In 2024–25, the DVA spent $3.5 billion on health, mainly on hospital services ($1.1 billion), primary health care ($1.1 billion) and referred medical services ($0.8 billion). DVA spending grew by 4.1% in real terms (Figure 13a). However, DVA changed its health expenditure reporting system in 2020–21, affecting the comparability of time series data. Results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Over the decade to 2024–25, DVA spending declined across the major health areas in real terms. Spending on public hospital services fell by an average of 8.1% per year, private hospitals by 5.2%, primary health care by 1.4% and referred medical services by 1.6%. In contrast, spending on other services, including patient transport, aids and appliances, and administration, increased by 7.0% per year on average. As noted above, changes to the reporting system may have affected growth rates over time.

Based on the DVA treatment population, which includes all Orange, Gold and White card holders, DVA spent $11,740 per person on health in 2024–25. This was 10.6% higher than average health spending across the Australian population ($10,614). Spending per DVA client peaked in 2014–15, declined until 2022–23, then rose slightly in 2023–24 and 2024–25 in real terms (Figure 13b).

This downward trend in the health spending per member of the DVA treatment population is due to the decline in the number of Veteran Gold Card Holders and increase in those of Veteran White Card Holders. DVA will pay for the hospital treatment costs for Veteran White Card holders for accepted conditions or conditions under non-liability health care whereas all hospital services that meet the clinical needs of Veteran Gold Card holders are paid by DVA.

Figure 13a: Department of Veterans’ Affairs health spending by area of spending, constant prices (a), 2014–15 to 2024–25

The line graph shows that Department of Veterans’ Affairs spent the most on primary health care and least on research. In 2024–25, $448 million was spent on public hospital services, $540 million on other services, $676 million on private hospitals, $790 million on referred medical services, and $1,054 million on primary health care.

Department of Defence health spending

In 2024–25, the Department of Defence (Joint Health Command) spent $779.1 million on health. This was an increase of 11.4% ($79.5 million) from 2023–24 in real terms. In 2024–25, the biggest area of spending was other health practitioner services ($242.6 million), followed by referred medical services ($160.5 million), unreferred medical services ($129.8 million), private hospitals ($89.3 million), dental services ($72.9 million) and all other medications ($17.1 million).

The amounts shown represent actual health expenditure by Defence for its ADF and APS employees that could be categorised as per AIHW’s area of expenditure classification, including direct spending on health care to members, direct costs of pharmaceuticals purchased by Defence and costs for administration, including the Defence electronic health record.

Note that it is not possible to reconcile this exactly against other departmental financial reporting because some expenditure within the Joint Health Command is not related to patient care and because of the accounting practices (for example, cost accrual) employed in departmental reporting. There are also areas of health expenditure within the Department that cannot be extracted from Departmental reporting such as building maintenance and other infrastructure costs, and material used within the operational environment.
