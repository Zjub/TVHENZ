e61 fiscal model - updated research prototype

Updated 27 October 2026. This version implements the first four development priorities from the original prototype and adds a dedicated Revenue tab. The previous code, inputs, README and PDF are preserved in archive/v1.

International debt comparisons: long-term IMF and OECD scenarios

The Fiscal outlook comparison starts in 2030. We use the PBO for our common FY2029-30 anchor and begin scenario changes afterwards. Short-run international forecasts and the previous near-term display option have been removed. No projection or fiscal parameter has been recalibrated to an international source. A display control switches between levels and changes from each series' own 2030 value; rebasing does not harmonise their accounting or policy assumptions.

The IMF does publish Australian long-term debt scenarios. These are in Country Report 26/38, Annex III, Figure 7, printed page 53 (PDF page 58). The report covers the 2025 Article IV consultation; its staff report was completed on 21 January 2026. This is a different source from the October 2026 Fiscal Monitor series used in the first version of this comparison, which ends in 2031.

Bottom-up construction

Spending now sums 24 subfunctions to the eight headline categories. Separate health age profiles and school/higher-education/VET demand replace the previous single future demand index for Health and Education. All 2030 category anchors and the NDIS and pension paths are preserved. Some blocks retain explicit simple proxies: residential/home care share an age profile and other social protection remains a working-age residual. No new causal pass-through estimate is claimed.

Health and education: differentiated mechanisms and evidence

The 27 October update replaces the common future Health and Education cost exponents with nine explicit subfunction specifications. It separates higher education from VET, bringing the model to 24 spending blocks. It does not fit nine causal elasticities or claim that the complete spending identity is observed. The purpose is to distinguish what can be measured from the assumptions needed to project it. The previous engine and allocations are preserved in archive/pre_health_education_2026_10_27.

Identity, units and interpretation

For these subfunctions the annual equation is:

spending[i,t] = anchor[i,2030]
               * population_index[t] * age_demand_index[i,t]
               * coverage_index[i,t] * intensity_index[i,t]
               * domestic_price_index[t] * real_unit_cost_index[i,t]
               * relative_service_price_index[i,t]
               * aggregate_cost_multiplier[function,t]

All indices equal one in 2030. The common domestic-price index serves as the model's CPI proxy, rather than a separately estimated Australian CPI path. Population times age demand equals the existing demographic exposure index. Coverage means access, eligibility or participation; intensity means services or inputs per covered person. Unit cost means government expense per modelled service unit, rather than the entire provider's costs in every case. In practice, the health age profiles are age-spending profiles: they already embed baseline differences in use, complexity, coverage and prices. They are not separate observations of each component. We hold that embedded pattern fixed and apply explicit changes to coverage and intensity afterwards. Consequently, the model is an anchored expenditure-index model; it does not yet forecast absolute numbers of hospital episodes or prescriptions.

Where an observed input share is available, the real unit-cost index is a base-period input basket:

real_unit_cost[i,t] = ((1 - labour_share[i])
                      + labour_share[i] * real_hourly_wage_index[t])
                     / (1 + service_productivity_growth[i])^(t-2030)
real_hourly_wage_index[t] = economy_wide_productivity_index[t]

The non-labour basket follows general domestic prices. Labour follows those prices plus economy-wide hourly productivity. Input quantities per service are fixed unless the service-productivity assumption changes. As relative wages rise, the labour share of the resulting cost basket rises: the formula is a fixed-quantity basket, not a constant-share Cobb-Douglas index. Linking wages to economy-wide productivity remains a structural assumption. An observed labour share alone says nothing about future service efficiency or the fiscal saving from better outcomes. Constant average hours does not prevent hourly wages from growing; the service-cost wage index does not use hours growth as a wage increase.

For VET the model instead compounds the annual funding proxy:

real_unit_cost[VET,t] = (1 + 0.6975 * annual_productivity_growth)^(t-2030)
                      / service_productivity_index[VET,t]

This is a weighted annual wage/CPI index under the model's wage assumption, not the fixed-quantity basket above. Medicines have a zero wage weight and a CPI-only unit-cost proxy. A common aggregate cost adjustment still applies to both VET and medicines.

Hospital calculation. IHACPA's NHCDC 2023-24 appendix workbook, sheet 5 Acute Overview, Appendix 5.2, national 2023-24 column, reports line-item costs per admitted-acute separation. The extractor sums the five salary/wage categories (nursing, medical non-VMO, medical VMO, allied health and other staff) and oncosts, then divides by the total line-item cost, including depreciation. The result is 0.6725298. The source covers public admitted-acute activity; our hospital block also includes other activity and government funding of private hospitals. This extension is a proxy, not a national hospital labour-share estimate. No recent one-year cost increase is extrapolated as long-run growth. IHACPA report and appendix downloads.

School calculation. ACARA's 2024 report, Table 9.3, supplies government-school recurrent spending for 2023/24. In $000, teaching salaries 31,447,043 plus non-teaching salaries 11,301,892 plus redundancies 19,259, divided by total recurrent expenditure excluding notional user cost of capital, 56,279,069, gives 0.7599307. Using the report's headline share including user cost of capital would give a different weight. We exclude that notional charge to improve alignment with the model's expense concept; this does not make the two accounting boundaries identical. Applying the government-school basket to all government school funding, including non-government-school grants, remains a proxy. ACARA Chapter 9, Table 9.3.

University calculation. Finance 2024 Table 4 reports employee benefits of $23,891.495 million and total expenses of $43,068.392 million. Their ratio is 0.5547339. These are whole-provider accounts, including research and activities funded by international fees and other private sources. We use the ratio as a cost-structure proxy for the higher-education block, not as a direct measurement of government-funded teaching costs or a rule for Commonwealth grants. Department of Education, Finance 2024, Table 4.

VET calculation. The National Skills Agreement specifies flexible-funding indexation using 85% WCI-1 and 15% WCI-6. Their wage weights are 75% and 40%, so the implied wage weight is 0.85*0.75 + 0.15*0.40 = 0.6975; the remaining 30.25% is CPI exposure. The agreement's wage component includes a productivity adjustment. We set that offset to zero in this prototype and disclose that choice; we do not claim to reproduce the complete index. Extending this Commonwealth funding formula to the whole VET allocation is also a proxy, not an observed provider input share or a consolidated current-policy projection. National Skills Agreement, flexible-funding indexation provisions.

Splitting the tertiary allocation without inventing a new total

ABS Education Table_1 reports consolidated tertiary operating expenses of $51.439bn in 2024-25, without the higher-education/VET split needed here. We allocate that control using two 2024 expenditure magnitudes: university total expenses of $43.068392bn and RoGS VET government recurrent expenditure excluding user cost of capital of $7.6172bn. The resulting VET allocation weight is 15.0283%; higher education receives the remainder. These are allocation weights, not additional spending. They sum back exactly to the existing ABS tertiary total. University accounts and VET recurrent expenditure differ in coverage and accounting; calendar 2024 also differs from financial 2024-25. The split is therefore explicitly classified as a cross-source proxy, pending a direct COFOG-compatible allocation. ABS Education workbook, RoGS 2026 VET, Table 5A.1.

The separate blocks use different age bases: 18-24 for higher education and 15-64 for VET. The VET age range follows the RoGS reporting scope, but uniform participation within that range is our assumption. The higher-education range remains a simple proxy, not a use of observed age-specific enrolment rates. Both omit changes in mature-age participation, international enrolments, course mix and study intensity. Other education still includes preschool within a population-based residual. These limitations are not resolved by having more rows.

The new cost specifications operate after 2030. The inherited 2025-to-2030 bridge still uses the earlier category cost assumptions to construct allocation weights, then reconciles to the unchanged eight category anchors. The separate VET age base changes within-education allocation weights slightly during that bridge; we do not claim every former subfunction anchor is identical. This is separate from the future input-cost mechanism and is visible in the calibration export.

Assumptions deliberately left unestimated

The parameter file sets annual coverage growth to zero and service-productivity growth to zero in all nine blocks. Health intensity remains 0.5% a year and education intensity remains zero. These are maintained scenario judgments, not new empirical findings. Baseline coverage and use are already embodied in the anchors and age profiles. We do not add another baseline take-up level on top.

The global 0.3% relative service-price premium remains an additional judgment. It applies to hospital, medical, residual health, school, university and residual education baskets. It is excluded from the CPI-only medicine proxy and the VET funding proxy, to avoid silently adding a second price rule to them. The health intensity assumption still applies to medicines: CPI-only unit cost does not mean CPI-only total spending. Demography, coverage, intensity and the category adjustment also affect the total. No generic substitution, new-listing or confidential rebate model has been fitted.

Primary/community and referred medical care retain the previous 70% wage-exposure judgment, interpreted now as an input-basket weight. We have not relabelled it as observed MBS policy. The PBO identifies WCI-5 as the MBS index and notes that parts of WCI construction are unpublished. Further separation of government rebates, community services, incentives and actual provider costs is necessary before claiming a faithful funding-rule model. PBO, Indexation and the budget: an introduction.

The apparent precision of a calculated salary share reflects source arithmetic, not certainty about its applicability for the next 36 years. Differences in efficiency, coverage or intensity are only introduced when a source or a stated scenario assumption supports them; no random subfunction growth rates are drawn.

Aggregate controls preserve the subfunction mechanisms

The sidebar retains its Cost pass-through section, but the first two controls are now Health: annual cost adjustment and Education: annual cost adjustment. Both default to 0%, range from -2% to +2%, and use:

aggregate_cost_multiplier[f,t] = (1 + annual_adjustment[f])^(t-2030)

For example, +1% gives a 2066 multiplier of 1.01^36 = 1.43077. Every unit-cost path in that function is multiplied by the same number, including medicines or VET. Thus relative subfunction costs at each date are preserved; neither the input weights nor demographic paths are overwritten. This is an annual growth adjustment, not a one-off 1% spending-level increase and not a percentage scaling of the productivity coefficient. It works even when productivity is zero and preserves the common 2030 anchor. A -1% adjustment gives 0.99^36.

The accounting decomposition adds Aggregate cost scaling as a separate tenth factor. The exact Shapley calculation allocates its interactions with the other factors and still sums to the spending change. Do not interpret its contribution as a causal estimate. The other four category pass-through controls retain their earlier definitions. References, resets and exports use the new zero-adjustment defaults; the old health/education slider values are not reused. The R engine rejects the obsolete health_pass_through and education_pass_through fields rather than silently ignoring them. Saved assumptions should use health_cost_growth_adjustment and education_cost_growth_adjustment, expressed as annual fractions (0.01 means 1%).

Differences from the IGR and remaining evidence work

This is a consolidated service-cost scenario framework. The IGR's main health programmes instead use programme-specific non-demographic growth estimated from historical age/sex-adjusted spending. Its Commonwealth education projections apply programme funding rules rather than our provider-cost baskets. A Commonwealth school-grant index is therefore not automatically the right rule for consolidated school delivery costs. The comparison panels retain those coverage and method differences rather than forcing the paths to coincide. 2026 IGR, Appendix A3, health and education methods.

The next data work is to estimate age-specific activity and participation, then separate changes in activity from expenditure per comparable service. Medicare and PBS data are useful for this, but they have not been fitted in this update. Their existence does not make the present 70% medical weight or CPI-only medicine assumption an estimate. In particular, benefits per MBS service and expenditure per prescription change with service/medicine mix as well as prices. Medicare demographic reports, PBS expenditure and prescriptions, higher-education student data, NCVER government-funded activity.

Fiscal coverage and reconciliation

PBO national totals already consolidate Commonwealth, state/territory and local general government. The model uses these totals directly. It does not sum gross Commonwealth and state budgets or count GST grants and NDIS funding contributions as extra national revenue or expenses.

consolidation_bridge.csv reports Commonwealth, state and national revenue and expenses. Its residual combines transfer elimination, local-government coverage and accounting adjustments; it is not a separately identified transfer total. The app displays this bridge. Independent long-run jurisdictional forecasts have not been constructed.

Detailed tax tables have a small revision/consolidation difference from national GFS. Their tax heads are reconciled proportionately to observed consolidated GFS taxation revenue. tax_consolidation_bridge.csv preserves both reported intergovernmental taxes and the actual net adjustment. GST is collected once in the Commonwealth tax data, with no state GST grant added.

PBO dollar amounts control the 2030 fiscal anchor. Nominal GDP is inferred from published gross debt dollars and its GDP ratio. Rounded reported ratios can therefore differ slightly from dollar-derived spending and revenue shares. The model preserves the dollar accounting identities rather than forcing all rounded ratios to agree simultaneously.

Spending allocation

2025 GFS primary expenses are allocated to eight modules: Health, Education, Age Pension, Aged Care, NDIS, Other Social Protection, Other Public Services and Defence. Conventional interest is removed once. The pension, aged-care and NDIS amounts are subtracted from Social Protection, not added above the GFS total. The broad functional mapping remains an approximation across programme and GFS reporting concepts; the reconciliation does not establish exact classification equivalence. NDIA administration stays in the residual public-service envelope.

Source exposures and default cost assumptions carry these allocations to 2030; one common factor reconciles them to PBO primary expenses. These are model allocations, not published PBO programme forecasts. Scenario changes never recalibrate this starting point. outputs/programme_calibration.csv exposes the 2025 values, raw calibration factor and 2030 amounts.

Demographics, participation and hours

IGR C8.6 publishes single-year age shares at 2025, 2036 and 2066. The model linearly interpolates those shares annually and normalises the published rounded shares. It chains annual IGR C8.1 population growth from the 2025 IGR population level. Centre for Population male/female ratios allocate each age to sex. The result is a reconciled age/sex input, not an independently estimated population cohort-component forecast. Rounded annual growth can produce a small difference from the IGR's rounded population-level chart.

ABS observed five-year age/sex participation and unemployment rates replace the old employment propensities. Annual participation probabilities shift in common log odds within each sex to reproduce IGR C9.13 structural participation targets. This is a transparent projection bridge; it does not reproduce Treasury's cohort participation model or independently identify future retirement behaviour.

Employment equals population times participation times one minus unemployment. Observed unemployment rates are held by age and sex. EM1a supplies actual hours per employed person, including non-working respondents. Detailed source rates and hours are available to 65+ as a combined group, so the model does not invent 75+ employment weights. Average hours per employed person follow the IGR C7.2 aggregate trend of -0.1% per year; the age/sex hour mix is rescaled to that target, avoiding a second composition effect. This constructs the source labour path. Future average hours use the separate control, constant by default, and population follows the chosen growth rate. Age-specific employment profiles and participation targets remain shared.

total hours = employed people * average weekly hours * 52
real output index = total hours index * productivity index
productivity index = (1 + selected productivity growth)^years

These are user-selected scenario settings, not a new economic forecast. The reference, initial controls and reset use these same defaults. Other defaults are wage tax indexation, 4% real super returns and a 0.3% service price premium.

population[t] = population[2030] * (1 + population growth)^t
NGDP per capita index[t] = (1 + selected per-capita growth)^t
NGDP[t] = NGDP[2030] * population index[t] * NGDP per capita index[t]
average hours[t] = average hours[2030] * (1 + hours growth)^t
real output index = employment index * average hours index * productivity index
domestic price index = NGDP index / (real output index * ToT income index)

Thus total nominal GDP grows at (1.032 * 1.012) - 1 = 4.4384% per year. Changing productivity or hours alone leaves every year's nominal GDP exactly unchanged. It changes real output and implied prices instead. Inflation is not merely per-capita nominal growth minus productivity: hours per resident also reflect participation, employment, ageing and average hours per worker.

The sidebar displays total NGDP growth, weekly hours at the anchor and endpoint, and implied inflation. Default average weekly hours remain about 31.66 in both 2030 and 2066. The common GDP anchor is unchanged; default 2066 GDP is $16.748tn. A 1.7% productivity scenario has precisely that same GDP when the other inputs are held fixed. The old mode switch and its hidden growth target are absent.

Population growth scales the sourced age/sex population cells uniformly within each year after 2030. The same cells feed employment, age-weighted service demand, NDIS cohorts and pension eligibility. Age shares continue to evolve along the sourced path; a different population growth setting does not simulate a different migration, fertility or mortality pattern. Observed employment rates and the IGR participation bridge are retained. A growing population raises both GDP and service demand; it need not raise spending/GDP proportionately.

The terms-of-trade setting remains a permanent level shift phased to 2040. Its 0.20 income elasticity is an illustrative assumption. At fixed nominal GDP per capita and population, domestic prices adjust to preserve nominal GDP; royalty exposure remains separate. The domestic price index is still a common CPI/wage-deflator proxy; the GDP deflator additionally includes the ToT effect. Hourly wages track prices and productivity. Annual earnings also include hours.

The common 2030 fiscal calibration and its historical 2025-30 carry-forward are unchanged. Only future paths use the new controls. Legacy ngdp_override and ngdp_growth arguments now fail explicitly, preventing silent use of an obsolete control. Age Pension, means tests and retirement incomes

The fixed annual coverage-decline parameter has been removed. The replacement module uses age 67 eligibility and calculates annual entitlements from explicit income and assets tests, taking the lower payment after those tests. Singles, couples, homeowners and non-homeowners have separate published free areas and maximum rates. Deeming uses the September 2026 1.75%/3.75% rates and thresholds. The combined couple income taper is 50 cents per dollar, or 25 cents per person; the asset taper is $3 per $1,000 per fortnight for the household.

Four wealth/income archetypes represent full-rate, assets-tested, income-tested and non-recipient cases. Their initial status weights come from the starting 2026 IGR C12.19 distribution. Future IGR coverage is retained as a benchmark, not fitted or imposed on this model. Representative assets and private incomes are constructed around the statutory thresholds; half-rate part-pension cases are assumed. Person weights of 35% single/65% couple members and 75% homeowner/ 25% non-homeowner are modelling judgements, not extracted household estimates.

For cohorts retiring after 2030, additional superannuation accumulates from 12% SG contributions, less a simplified 15% contributions tax, using the ATO mean taxable income as an earnings proxy and the selected real return (default 4%, selectable from 0% to 7% in half-percentage-point steps, including 6.5%). Balances earn returns and use a stylised 5% drawdown after retirement. This is not a reconstruction of full past work histories, contribution caps, housing wealth, residence eligibility, Work Bonus, partner-age differences or observed household wealth distributions. It improves the mechanism while leaving substantial pension calibration work before publication.

The real-return control operates only through private retirement assets and Age Pension means tests. Higher returns increase accumulated contributions and slow asset depletion after retirement. Larger assessable assets and deemed income reduce pension eligibility/payments; smaller deficits then reduce debt and subsequent interest costs. Deeming rates do not automatically follow the chosen return. GDP, government borrowing rates, public asset yields and the superannuation-tax revenue block do not change directly. In particular, the model does not capture extra earnings-tax receipts from higher fund returns.

Observed allocations and the common anchor

Fixed 42/20/25 percent revenue weights are removed. ABS observations identify personal income tax, company tax, superannuation tax, Medicare/FBT, GST, excises and customs, payroll, property, stamp duties and other taxes. The app's detailed revenue table and downloads expose each head rather than treating all receipts as a fixed GDP share.

Personal income tax and threshold policy

ATO Table 16A supplies counts and taxable income by percentile, sex and state. The model aggregates mutually exclusive sex/state cells into 100 percentile cells and applies the resident schedule to each cell's mean taxable income. It does not sum published subtotal rows. The distribution is uprated from 2023-24 to 2030; future per-taxpayer income follows average nominal earnings, and taxpayer numbers scale with employment. This assumes a stable relative income distribution and does not independently model pensioner, non-resident or capital-gains tax bases.

The 2030 schedule uses thresholds $18,200/$45,000/$135,000/$190,000 and marginal rates 0/14/30/37/45%, including the legislated 2027-28 lower rate. LITO is explicit. Medicare and FBT are modelled in their separate observed tax block; income-tax outputs are not incorrectly labelled as including those levies. The WATO offset, whose wage-only eligibility cannot be identified from Table 16, is not simulated. Its starting aggregate effect and other differences enter the fiscal calibration.

Three annual threshold conventions are selectable from 2030:

  • Fixed nominal: unchanged nominal thresholds and offset amounts. This corresponds to the statutory threshold convention, without assumed future cuts.
  • CPI indexed: thresholds and LITO amounts increase with domestic prices.
  • Wage indexed: thresholds and LITO amounts increase with average earnings.

Wage indexation is the default policy assumption, including on reset and in the fixed reference. It removes bracket creep from growth in average earnings. CPI indexation remains selectable and retains bracket creep from real earnings growth. Wage indexation is not current legislation; fixed nominal thresholds remain a selectable statutory-convention alternative. The tax schedule itself generates the changing effective rate; there is no 1.1 proxy income-tax elasticity. The app shows the direct revenue difference relative to wage indexation under the same economic scenario. That comparison does not re-simulate debt and interest receipts, which would require a complete financing counterfactual. No revenue/GDP cap or hidden tax relief is applied.

Structural company tax

Company tax is projected as a structural share of GDP, with no business-cycle mechanism and no historical nominal-GDP elasticity:

company tax/GDP = calibrated 2030 company-tax/GDP share * ToT index^eta
company tax dollars = nominal GDP * company tax/GDP

The starting share is 4.9026% of GDP, taken from the existing calibrated 2030 allocation. This is a modelling anchor, not a freshly estimated normal-profit share or a separately published PBO company-tax forecast. eta, the elasticity of the GDP share to the ToT level, defaults to 1 and can be varied from 0 to 2 under advanced assumptions. Higher nominal growth at unchanged real assumptions should not itself change these tax/GDP paths under indexed policy. Evidence, judgments and structural implications

data/assumption_register.csv is the full, downloadable audit register, shown under Method & data in the app. Rebuild it offline with Rscript scripts/build_assumption_register.R. It records each choice's value or rule, classification, named source and URL, our addition and its limitation. The register is a snapshot of the default model; scenario selections are exported separately in the assumptions download.

  • Sourced: an observed figure, published forecast or policy parameter directly transcribed. It may still be applied to a broader population than its source.
  • Calibrated: a model-derived allocation reconciled to published totals. A PBO aggregate anchor does not make individual programme or tax-head allocations official PBO forecasts.
  • Proxy: an unavailable economic base represented by another series, with an explicit maintained relationship. For example, constant consumption/GDP.
  • Judgment / user choice: a scenario convention or guess without an estimated supporting coefficient. A source cited for the starting value does not support the guessed future response.
  • Identity: a result that follows algebraically from equations. Its economic realism still depends on the chosen equations and their assumptions.

Several entries combine these classes: a sourced age-cost profile and an assumed cost-growth rate play different roles within the same spending module.

domestic prices = nominal GDP index / (real output index * ToT income factor)
service spending = anchor * exposure * coverage * domestic prices * real unit cost
                   * relative-price factor * intensity * aggregate cost scaling

At fixed real assumptions and ToT, higher NGDP/person raises domestic prices, nominal GDP and indexed service spending proportionately. Dividing spending by GDP cancels the nominal change. ToT is different: at fixed GDP it changes the real-income factor, hence domestic prices and spending/GDP. Raising NGDP does not reverse that wedge merely by changing the overall nominal scale.

For example, in 2066 at differentiated default costs: Health is $1,615.4bn (9.646% GDP) with unchanged ToT and NGDP/person growth 3.2%. Raising NGDP/person growth to 4% then raises Health to $2,056.8bn and GDP to $22,114.9bn; Health remains 9.300% of GDP. Thus the dollar link works even though the share is unchanged. Debt and financial assets

All spending and tax cases use the same financing engine. Interest is charged on opening gross debt. Its effective rate starts at the PBO 2030 bill divided by 2029 gross debt and converges by 2040 to the selected rate, 4.4% by default. Interest receipts use opening financial assets and the anchor-calibrated yield.

Assets maintain the initial stock per person in domestic purchasing power; acquisition is explicitly financed. After gross debt is repaid, further surpluses accumulate in interest-bearing assets. Net debt may be negative.

fiscal balance = revenue - expenses - net capital investment
gross debt change = -fiscal balance + financial asset acquisition
net debt = gross debt - financial assets

There is no automatic fiscal repair. Revaluation, maturity buckets, indexed bonds and a full public-sector balance sheet remain outside scope. Recursive identities apply from 2031; published historical/PBO stocks are not reconstructed from rounded historical flow ratios.

Understanding debt and interest under the new controls

The Debt & interest tab shows gross debt, modelled financial assets and net debt, plus interest paid, interest received and net interest. Charts switch between AUD billions and GDP shares. Tables expose opening debt, borrowing rate, asset yield, GDP and the implied real borrowing rate. Downloads contain annual detail.

At the differentiated wage-indexed defaults, 2066 net debt is 112.28% GDP and interest paid is 5.19% GDP. With productivity at 1.7% and all other inputs unchanged, net debt is 88.97% and interest paid 4.16% GDP. Nominal GDP is $16.748tn in both scenarios. The default debt level is higher than under CPI indexation because the model no longer collects additional personal tax through real bracket creep. These are conditional scenario outcomes, not an accounting correction or forecast. These outcomes are illustrative and depend on the spending and revenue rules.

net debt = gross debt - modelled financial assets
interest paid[t] = nominal borrowing rate[t] * opening gross debt[t]
interest received[t] = calibrated asset yield * opening financial assets[t]
net interest = interest paid - interest received
interest paid/GDP = dollar interest paid / current nominal GDP

A lower net-debt figure alone does not determine the gross interest bill. The opening gross stock, nominal borrowing rate and GDP denominator must be compared. The nominal rate remains independent of implied inflation: higher productivity at fixed nominal income lowers inflation and raises the real borrowing rate. There is no automatic Fisher adjustment, maturity structure or monetary-policy reaction. Financial assets earn a single calibrated yield; this is a simplified balance sheet. The reference remains fixed at all default assumptions.

These checks test internal restrictions; they do not establish that the proxy assumptions are good empirical forecasts. tests/browser_smoke.R checks live controls, tabs, reset and downloads. tests/validate_indexation.R additionally verifies that CPI-indexed and fixed nominal thresholds change scenario net debt while the reference and its exported series stay fixed. It checks the policy contribution and mixed-control attribution. The browser regression checks both the net-debt headline and its reference gap.

Main files:

  • R/model.R: public entry point; R/model_v2.R: projection and mechanism code.
  • scripts/acquire_update_sources.R: cache sources and archive the first version.
  • scripts/prepare_update_inputs.R: reproducible data extraction and reconciliation.
  • data/raw/v2: raw workbooks, reports, chart data and request manifest.
  • data/processed: model inputs, tax elasticity estimates, coverage bridges, IGR Commonwealth benchmarks and source checksums.
  • data/policy_parameters.csv and data/ndis_incidence_2025.csv: explicit manually transcribed anchors/rules with provenance. Old top-level CSVs not loaded by v2 are retained for traceability; the active inputs are named in load_inputs().
  • data/assumption_register.csv: source, value/rule, classification, model addition and limitation for each material input or rule. Rebuilt offline by scripts/build_assumption_register.R; available in the app and its download.
  • outputs/reference_projection.csv, reference_components.csv, reference_revenue.csv, reference_revenue_diagnostics.csv: default results.
  • outputs/programme_calibration.csv, labour_projection.csv, ndis_participant_projection.csv, pension_projection.csv: mechanism diagnostics.
  • outputs/validation_checks.csv, browser_smoke_results.txt and screenshots: verification evidence. Logs and PID identify the local Shiny process.
  • README.pdf: printable version, regenerated by scripts/render_readme_pdf.R.

Next development priorities

The first four priorities now have working implementations, with the remaining qualifications above. The next priorities are:

  1. Backcast programme spending, taxes and balances and assess historical forecast vintages. Use the existing top-down model as an independent challenger.
  2. Replace synthetic pension wealth and family/tenure weights with household or administrative distributions; improve contribution histories, drawdowns and exact indexation before calling the pension module a current-policy forecast.
  3. Add separately estimated service productivity, disability-specific NDIS costs, residential/home-care demand, education enrolment and current-policy reforms.
  4. Improve tax bases, offsets, state thresholds, excise erosion and jurisdictional budgets; quantify structural and policy uncertainty before public release.
