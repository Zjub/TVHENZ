                                            ABSTRACT/R<U+00C9>SUM<U+00C9>
                  Illustrating long-run fiscal challenges using the Long-Term Model
This paper presents updated estimates of long-run fiscal gaps for 35 OECD and accession candidate
countries using the OECD Global Long-Term Model. The long-run fiscal gap measures the fiscal
adjustment required at the 2060 horizon to keep public debt ratios stable near current levels while
maintaining current service standards and benefit entitlements. The methodology incorporates several
refinements, including projected increases in defence spending and a more detailed treatment of primary
expenditure. In the absence of additional policy action, the median country would require a fiscal
adjustment of 4.7 percentage points of GDP between 2027 and 2060 to stabilise debt, with substantially
larger adjustments in some countries. Population ageing is the main driver of long-run fiscal pressures
through higher pension, health and long-term care spending, compounded in many cases by weak initial
fiscal positions. The paper also assesses the sensitivity of fiscal gaps to key assumptions and policy
scenarios.
Keywords: long-term projection, long-run fiscal gap, fiscal sustainability, fiscal pressure

    Main findings
    This paper presents the latest estimates of long-run fiscal gaps based on the OECD Global Long-Term
    Model. It describes the current methodology in detail and assesses the indicator<U+2019>s sensitivity to key
    assumptions, covering 35 OECD and accession candidate countries. This version introduces several
    refinements to the previous methodology, including explicit accounting for projected increases in
    defence expenditure, a more detailed treatment of current primary expenditure, and the inclusion of
    revaluation effects on government financial assets. The analysis provides illustrative estimates of future
    fiscal challenges across countries using a common stylised framework. Given this, individual-country
    results should not be viewed as precise quantifications. The main findings are:
       <U+2022>   In the absence of further policy changes, maintaining current service standards and benefits
           while keeping the gross public debt ratio stable would require a fiscal adjustment of 4.7
           percentage points (pp) of GDP between 2027 and 2060 in the median country. In ten countries,
           the required fiscal adjustment exceeds 8 pp of GDP.
       <U+2022>   The main sources of long-run fiscal gaps are weak initial fiscal positions relative to those that
           would keep government debt-to-GDP ratios stable (0.9 pp of GDP in the median country), and
           population ageing, which raises public expenditure on both pensions (2.2 pp) as well as health
           and long-term care (1.8 pp).
       <U+2022>   Substantial long-run fiscal gaps imply the need for higher revenue or commensurate
           expenditure restraint to prevent rising government debt ratios. Without revenue adjustments or
           spending reductions, government net debt could rise by 43 pp of GDP by 2045 in the median
           country, and by up to 124 pp in the United States.
       <U+2022>   The required fiscal adjustment through 2060 is marginal (<0.5 pp of GDP) or negative in three
           countries <U+2014> Denmark, Iceland and Ireland <U+2014> reflecting relatively strong initial fiscal positions

1. Introduction

Most OECD governments face long-run fiscal challenges due to weak initial fiscal positions, rising financing
costs, slowing economic growth, and mounting spending pressures linked to population ageing and the
increasing relative price of government-provided services. The magnitude of this challenge can be
illustrated with long-run fiscal gap estimates from the OECD Global Long-Term Model (LTM).
This indicator, previously called <U+201C>long-run fiscal pressure<U+201D>, first appeared in Guillemette and Turner
(2018[1]). The methods for projecting certain fiscal components and the way the overall long-run fiscal gap
is decomposed have evolved subsequently, for instance separating out defence expenditure and
projecting it explicitly. In addition to presenting the latest estimates, this paper describes the current
methodology in detail and assesses the indicator<U+2019>s sensitivity to key assumptions. It also updates estimates
of capital costs for new electricity generation previously published in Guillemette and Ch<U+00E2>teau (2023[2]), a

potential additional fiscal challenge not included in the standard indicator. Although the LTM horizon is now
2100, the analysis in this note runs to 2060 to be comparable to earlier work.
The scenarios and fiscal projections in this note are intended to illustrate broad trends and support
discussion of future fiscal challenges. They aim to provide approximate orders of magnitude and, by
applying a common and relatively simple framework across countries, enable meaningful cross-country
comparisons. However, the results should not be viewed as precise estimates for individual countries. The
LTM relies largely on stylised assumptions and reduced-form equations and omits many country-specific
factors, a necessary trade-off for generating scenarios across numerous economies. Such factors may
include country-specific institutional arrangements or projected balance-sheet developments, such as the
accumulation of assets in funded public pension plans. The LTM also does not account for potential
shorter-term macroeconomic responses to fiscal actions. Moreover, uncertainty surrounding multi-decade
projections is inherently large. Some sources of uncertainty are illustrated below, but the true extent,
including unquantifiable risks, is likely to be substantially greater.
Broader analyses of long-run fiscal challenges and policy implications can be found in Rouzet et al.
(2019[3]), Crowe et al. (2022[4]), Koutsogeorgopoulou and Morgavi (2025[5]) and OECD (2025[6]). In addition,
OECD Economic Surveys provide extensive country-specific analysis of short and long-run fiscal
challenges and policy recommendations. For instance, the potential short-run impact of sustained fiscal
action is discussed in some recent Country Surveys (OECD, 2024[7]; OECD, 2024[8]).
The note is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the latest estimates of long-run fiscal gaps. Section
3 examines the sensitivity of these estimates to major assumptions underlying the long-term scenarios.
Section 4 considers long-run fiscal sustainability from a different angle by projecting government debt-to-
GDP ratios and gross financing needs. Finally, Section 5 updates previously published estimates of
potential capital costs associated with building new electricity-generation capacity in both the baseline and
an energy-transition scenario.


2. The long-run fiscal gap indicator and its components

        The long-term model and the fiscal closure rule

The LTM is a country-level model with an annual frequency, comprising dynamic projection rules and
identities for key variables over a horizon to 2100. For each country, the model<U+2019>s core is a production
function with standard inputs <U+2014> labour and capital <U+2014> and labour efficiency. The LTM covers 139 individual
countries, with a further 55 countries grouped into three regional aggregates. Most variables are anchored
to the latest OECD Economic Outlook; for this note, the starting point is the OECD Economic Outlook 119
database of June 2026, with projections until 2027.
The model includes a <U+201C>fiscal block<U+201D> that projects government primary expenditure by function, interest
payments and receipts, and the evolution of government financial assets and liabilities. This block is
available for 33 OECD countries (Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico and T<U+00FC>rkiye are excluded due to
incomplete comparable fiscal data) and three accession candidate countries (Bulgaria, Croatia, and
Romania). Norway is typically excluded from cross-country comparisons because of its exceptionally large
net financial asset position, although a modified fiscal block is available for more tailored analyses. This
note therefore focuses on the remaining 35 countries for which a fiscal block exists in the LTM. Future
work could aim to expand fiscal projections and the long-run pressure indicators to a larger number of
countries.
In a multi-decade model, a technical requirement is that the government debt-to-GDP ratio must eventually
stabilise. For this purpose, the model includes a reaction function that dynamically adjusts the ratio of
cyclically adjusted primary revenue to potential GDP to stabilise the gross debt ratio at its value in the final



                       ILLUSTRATING LONG-RUN FISCAL CHALLENGES USING THE LONG-TERM MODEL <U+00A9> OECD 2026

                                                                                                              |9

year of the OECD Economic Outlook projection (2027). This also implies stabilising the net government
debt-to-GDP ratio given the LTM assumption that government financial assets remain stable as a share of
GDP (see section 2.4). The projected change in the revenue ratio over a long horizon is referred to as the
long-run fiscal gap or, interchangeably, the required long-run fiscal adjustment. It provides a benchmark
metric of the fiscal challenge over the long run, accounting for the initial fiscal position as well as projected
growth, interest rates, and expenditure trajectories.
The use of a revenue measure for debt stabilisation in the LTM is a technical modelling choice, not a policy
prescription. The appropriate policy mix will differ across countries and may include revenue-raising
measures alongside expenditure reforms, pension and health reforms, growth-enhancing reforms, and in
some cases additional debt financing. Other considerations not captured in the LTM framework, such as
distributional impacts, may also inform policy choices.
The long-run fiscal gap indicator has several advantages:
    <U+2022>   It is defined in structural terms, adjusting for cyclical or temporary components in initial fiscal
        positions, despite uncertainties in such estimates.
    <U+2022>   It is forward-looking, accounting for projected interest rate and growth trajectories.
    <U+2022>   It avoids categorising fiscal positions as definitively sustainable or unsustainable <U+2014> an assessment
        that cannot be made reliably given the inherent uncertainty of the future (Blanchard, Leandro and
        Zettelmeyer, 2021[9]).
The LTM approach allows very different government debt ratios across countries to persist. Alternative
approaches could allow some increase in debt ratios for the lower-debt countries, or enforce reductions in
debt ratios for highly indebted ones. In either case, the medium-term fiscal adjustment path would differ,
but the size of the long-run adjustment would be little affected (see section 3.2). Another potential issue is
that the long-run fiscal gap considers only the impact of population ageing on the spending side of the
fiscal ledger, but not the revenue side. Recent OECD research has shown that population ageing could
depress the tax-to-GDP ratio by 1 percentage point on average by 2060, mostly via a reduction in labour
income taxes (Sicsic and Hourani, 2026[10]). Although modest relative to the impact of ageing on spending,
such changes represent an additional long-term fiscal gap that may need closing with policy measures
such as base broadening or tax rate changes.
The LTM-based fiscal adjustment begins in 2028 and comes in addition to any projected fiscal adjustment
in 2026 and 2027 in the OECD Economic Outlook 119 database. The adjustment between 2025 and 2027
is small in the median country but exceeds <U+00BD> per cent of GDP in 13 countries (Figure 1). Thereafter, an
annual cap of one percentage point of potential GDP is imposed on the fiscal adjustment to reflect political-
economy constraints. While the one-percentage-point limit is somewhat arbitrary, larger sustained
adjustments have been comparatively rare. The constraint implies that it can take a few years for the
objective of a stable debt ratio at target to be achieved in countries where the initial fiscal position is very
far from the level required to stabilise the debt ratio.

The LTM-based fiscal trajectories and the annual cap on the size of the fiscal adjustment may diverge from
current government plans and may differ from national or supranational fiscal objectives, targets, or rules.
Moreover, the model does not account for potential short-term demand effects of fiscal consolidation or for
negative supply-side feedback from higher taxation. The gap between actual and cyclically-adjusted
revenue is determined by the output gap <U+2014> assumed to close gradually over a few years <U+2014> and by a
country-specific semi-elasticity coefficient estimated in earlier OECD work (Price, Dang and Botev,
2015[11]).

          Long-run fiscal gaps

In the standard business-as-usual scenario, the long-run fiscal gap is 4.7 percentage points (pp) of GDP
in the median country at the 2060 horizon (Figure 2). In ten countries, the gap exceeds 8 pp. It is marginal
(<0.5 pp) or negative in only three countries <U+2014> Denmark, Iceland and Ireland <U+2014> reflecting relatively strong
initial fiscal positions as well as low or negative net public debt, and in the case of Denmark future statutory
retirement ages that are linked to life expectancy. The time profile of the fiscal gap for the median country
is somewhat front-loaded, with about 2.6 of the 4.7 pp long-run fiscal adjustment being implemented in the
first decade of the projection period, in large part because of a median initial structural fiscal gap of nearly
1 pp of GDP (Figure 3).

The long-run fiscal gap can be decomposed into several components with intuitive interpretations (see
Annex A for the algebraic decomposition). The following subsections look at each in turn, decomposing its
drivers where feasible.

          The initial structural fiscal gap

The initial structural fiscal gap measures the difference between the structural primary balance in 2027
and that which would stabilise the debt-to-GDP ratio at its 2027 level at the growth and interest rates
projected to prevail that year. It is 0.9 pp in the median country, though variation across countries is
substantial. Four countries, including the United States, show an initial structural fiscal gap of 2 pp of GDP
or more (Figures 2 and 4).

           The interest rate <U+2013> growth differential

In the LTM, the short-term real interest rate converges to a neutral rate, assumed to equal trend economic
growth, while inflation stabilises at an equilibrium rate embedded in nominal interest rates. 2 Consequently,
short-term nominal rates converge to nominal potential growth on a country-specific basis, except in euro
area countries, where they converge to euro area-wide potential growth. Long-term nominal interest rates
(10-year government bond yields) are modelled as forward convolutions of projected short-term rates, plus
a uniform 70 basis point term premium 3 and a country-specific fiscal risk premium based on fiscal positions
and other structural factors. 4 The modelling assumptions imply that both short and long-term market
interest rates react one-for-one to changes in potential growth rates, all else equal, after some adjustment
period.

One important feature of long-run government expenditure projections is the overall degree of pass-
through from economy-wide real wage growth into spending levels, which reflects the different degrees of
wage indexation assumed for the various spending components. Zero pass-through (purely price indexing)
would imply that public sector wages, benefits and social transfers would not keep pace with trend growth
in private-sector wages, and thus that public sector workers and benefit recipients would tend to become
relatively poorer over time. Full pass-through would imply keeping perfect pace with the private sector but
government spending is not composed only of wages and transfers, it also consists of goods and services
purchased on the private market which should naturally follow a price as opposed to a wage index. The
degree of indexation for overall government spending should thus intuitively fall somewhere between zero
and one.
For defence, net capital expenditure and parts of other current primary expenditure, the volume and price
assumptions cannot be separated, because LTM projection rules are expressed directly in percentage of
GDP terms. However, assuming that projected changes in these expenditure categories are driven by
volume rather than relative price effects and weighing the other categories by their relative GDP shares
(column 2 of Table 2), then the overall degree of real wage indexation for total primary expenditure in the
LTM works out to approximately 54% in the median country. By design, this is approximately the share of
expenditure attributable to compensation of employees and cash social transfers in the median OECD
country.

           Health and long-term care expenditure

In the LTM, growth in real per capita public expenditure on health and long-term care (measured relative
to the GDP deflator) is based on the work of Farag et al. (2012[14]) and Lorenzoni et al. (2019[15]) and driven
by three factors: real GDP per capita growth (the income effect), changes in the share of the population
aged 65 and over (the ageing effect) and the excess of health-care inflation over general inflation (the
Baumol effect). 8 The first two factors primarily influence the volume of services, while the Baumol effect
influences their relative price, reflecting the cost pressures typical of labour-intensive sectors where
productivity grows more slowly than in the wider economy. The relative price assumption is that, in addition
to the general price level (GDP deflator), health and long-term care prices are assumed to increase by one
half of economy-wide real wage growth, which is equal to economy-wide labour productivity growth by
construction. Given that compensation of employees accounts for 30% to 50% of health spending in OECD
countries once accounting for wages embedded in purchased services (intermediate consumption), the
assumption implies that wages in this sector rise in line with economy-wide wages. It also implies that
faster productivity growth magnifies the Baumol effect.
Under a business-as-usual assumption in which no major policy reforms are implemented, real per-capita
public expenditure on health and long-term care is projected to rise by 1.8 pp of GDP between 2027 and
2060 in the median country (Figure 11). Most of this increase is driven by income growth; roughly one-third
reflects the Baumol effect; and slightly less than one-fifth arises from population ageing. The ageing effect
varies widely across countries depending on demographic trends <U+2014> for example, it is nearly three times
larger in Korea than in Denmark. The Baumol effect is stronger in countries that have lower per capita
incomes today because of their higher projected productivity growth through 2060.

         Pension expenditure

Public pension expenditure (defined as cash expenditure on old-age and survivors<U+2019> pensions) is projected
in the LTM based on the change in the ratio of retirees to workers and the evolution of the average benefit
ratio (the ratio of the average public pension benefit to the average wage). It is projected to increase by
2.2 pp of GDP in the median country between 2027 and 2060 (Figure 2, Panel A). Projected increases in
public pension expenditure tend to be smaller in countries that have legislated increases in statutory
retirement ages, especially those linking future retirement ages to life expectancy (e.g. Finland), and larger
in countries with low statutory retirement ages and particularly unfavourable demographic profiles (e.g.
Poland).
In the business-as-usual scenario, country-specific average pension benefit ratios are assumed to remain
constant over the projection period, with pensions rising in line with the average wage. However, in some
countries, existing pension legislation implies a substantial reduction in benefit ratios over time, due to
indexation to price inflation (e.g. France), or to a mixed index of price and wage inflation (e.g. Finland,
Switzerland) or through other changes to pension entitlements such as those for new entrants (e.g. Italy).
As a result, the LTM may overstate the increase in pension expenditure if these policies remain unchanged.
The LTM approach also differs markedly from the European Commission<U+2019>s central scenario, which reflects
legislated pension system parameters, including affordability rules and other mechanisms that, if
unchanged, imply an 8.4 pp decline in the average benefit ratio at the EU level between 2025 and 2060
(European Commission, 2024[18]).

The constant-benefit-ratio assumption in the LTM is motivated by two main considerations:
     <U+2022>    Simplicity. It can be applied uniformly across EU and non-EU countries, whereas setting country-
          specific assumptions for non-EU countries would be difficult without comparable official projections.
     <U+2022>    Plausibility. A continuous decline in average pension benefit ratios over several decades, as less
          than full wage indexation would imply, seems unlikely given the salience of inequality and old-age
          poverty and the growing political weight of older voters. In practice, governments often introduce
          ad hoc adjustments to maintain pensioners<U+2019> relative purchasing power. This pattern is reflected in
          historical outcomes. For example, the 2015 Ageing Report projected a 1.9 pp decline between
          2013 and 2025 (European Commission, 2015[19]), whereas the EU average benefit ratio has risen
          since 2013, as well as in years prior (Figure 12). This suggests an effective link between wages
          and average pension benefits. Assuming this link persists provides a more realistic foundation for
          long-run fiscal analysis, while alternative policy choices regarding pension generosity can be
          explored in scenario variants (see section 3.5).

Defence expenditure is incorporated explicitly in the long-run fiscal gap indicator for the first time in this

    <U+2022>    Non-NATO members: Defence expenditure is assumed to return to its 15-year historical average

             Other current primary expenditure

The approach to projecting other current primary expenditure has been refined from previous versions of
the LTM to better reflect the composition of other primary government expenditure in the average OECD
country. The previous approach was based on two simplifying assumptions regarding the volume and
relative price of government services in this expenditure item: 10
      <U+2022>    Volume component: Real per capita spending in this category was assumed to remain constant
           over time. Accordingly, the volume of government services was projected to grow in line with total
           population.
      <U+2022>    Price component: The price of government services was assumed to increase at the rate of nominal
           wages.
Under these assumptions, changes in the ratio of other current primary expenditure to GDP depended
solely on the population-to-employment ratio. As a result, productivity-enhancing reforms had no long-run
impact on this spending category as a share of GDP.
In practice, while full wage indexation is appropriate for major components such as compensation of
employees and social transfers, other items are more appropriately linked to the general price level. These
include parts of intermediate consumption and purchased market output (e.g. social transfers in kind) that
reflect competitively supplied goods and services (such as electricity, fuel, and office equipment), as well
as subsidies. In these areas, productivity gains can cause spending to decline as a share of GDP. Indexing
these items to prices rather than wages implies that their share of GDP declines as economy-wide
productivity increases.
The revised approach is this paper distinguishes four expenditure categories based on COFOG data, each
with specific assumptions regarding volume and price developments (Table 3):
      1. Education (22% of other current primary expenditure): The volume component is assumed to
         evolve in proportion to the population aged 5 to 24. The price component is assumed to increase
         with the GDP deflator plus two-thirds of real wage growth. This implies a gradual rise in the relative
         price of education services, reflecting a Baumol-type effect similar to that assumed for health and
         long-term care, but with a higher coefficient (two-thirds rather than one-half) to reflect this sector<U+2019>s
         higher labour intensity. This allows salaries in the education sector to rise in line with economy-
         wide wages.
      2. Social protection, excluding old-age and survivors<U+2019> benefits (31%): The volume component is
         assumed to grow in line with the total population. The price component follows the GDP deflator
         plus real wage growth, implying that the average benefit ratio (average benefit relative to the
         average wage) remains constant over time. This ensures consistency with the treatment of public
         pension expenditure.
      3. Housing and community amenities (2%): The volume component is assumed to grow with the total
         population, while the price component follows the GDP deflator only.
      4. Other categories (45%): Expenditure in the remaining categories, including for example general
         public services and environmental protection, is assumed to remain constant as a share of nominal
         GDP. For these items, distinguishing between volume and price components is conceptually
         difficult and a constant-share-of-GDP assumption is consistent with long-term trends in countries
         with available historical data.

categories. Instead, total expenditure growth is projected as a weighted average of the volume and price
determinants described above. The shares are based on unweighted OECD averages, assumed to be

3. Some sensitivity analyses

This section looks at the sensitivity of the long-run fiscal gap estimates to some of the underlying
assumptions and trends.

         Sensitivity to interest rates

Given the substantial uncertainty surrounding the future path of interest rates, two alternative scenarios
assess the impact of lower and higher interest rate trajectories relative to the baseline. These interest rate
shocks are introduced by adding one percentage point (positive-risk scenario) or subtracting one
percentage point (negative-risk scenario) from the equilibrium short-term neutral interest rate in all
countries. The shocks feed quickly into short- and long-term market interest rates, and more gradually into
implicit average interest rates on government debt (depending on country-specific debt maturity structures)
and on the rate of return on government financial assets, as previously explained.
Higher interest rates than in the baseline scenario increase the long-run fiscal gap, while lower rates reduce
it (Figure 20). Unsurprisingly, a country<U+2019>s gross government debt position is the dominant factor shaping
its sensitivity to interest rate shocks. In the higher-interest-rate scenario, long-run fiscal gaps increase by
as much as 1-1<U+00BC> pp of GDP in countries with the largest debt burdens, such as Greece, Italy and Japan.
The pattern is reversed in the lower-interest-rate scenario, although the same countries remain the most
affected.

While changing the debt target has little effect on the long-run fiscal gap, it strongly influences the interim
fiscal trajectory. Reducing the debt ratio requires not only closing the initial structural fiscal gap and
offsetting rising expenditure, as in the baseline, but also sustaining a sufficiently strong fiscal balance to

consolidation pace, and implementation constraints. A front-loaded adjustment produces an early steep
improvement in the fiscal balance followed by a decline, while others smooth the adjustment over a longer
period at a lower peak.
Regardless of the path, a much larger fiscal adjustment than in the baseline scenario would be needed
over an extended interim period. In the Belgium example, the model<U+2019>s revenue instrument is 2.9 pp of GDP
above baseline over the 2028-45 period, peaking at 6.2 pp above baseline (Figure 21, Panel B). While the
exact numbers and the fiscal instrument used are illustrative, the key takeaway is that achieving large debt
reductions would require a substantial fiscal effort over the coming decades in most countries.

         Sensitivity to growth rates and the sources of growth

Boosting growth could help narrow fiscal deficits and improve long-run fiscal sustainability, although it is
unlikely to eliminate the need for fiscal adjustment entirely. To assess the sensitivity of long-run fiscal gap
estimates to stronger growth, two scenarios examine the effects of successful reforms that entail no direct
fiscal costs, thereby isolating the pure impact of higher growth. In both scenarios, average annual potential
growth rises by around 0.3 percentage points between 2028 and 2060, resulting in a cumulative increase
of about 10% in the level of potential output by 2060. In the first scenario, this increase is driven by a 10%
rise in trend labour efficiency; in the second, by a 10% increase in employment (with the working-age
population unchanged, implying higher employment rates). In both cases, reforms raise potential growth
in a hump-shaped pattern relative to baseline, consistent with the treatment of structural reforms in the
LTM: potential growth gradually increases before returning to baseline.
The fiscal effects differ markedly across the two scenarios. In the higher labour-efficiency scenario, rising
incomes increase health and long-term care spending by around 0.2 pp of GDP by 2060. At the same time,
other primary expenditure declines by a broadly similar magnitude as a share of GDP because wage
growth is only partially passed through into government spending (see section 2). As a result, the long-run
fiscal gap for the median country remains broadly unchanged relative to baseline (Figure 22), although in

         Sensitivity to net international migration

In the UNPD population projections used in the baseline scenario, country-level net international migration
remains close to recent levels up to 2060. However, future population movements and migration policies
are highly uncertain. To assess the sensitivity of long-run fiscal gap estimates to migration, an alternative
scenario is run in which net migration in OECD countries rises over the baseline level by 0.1% of the
current total population by 2033 and remains at that higher level thereafter, thus raising total population by
about 3<U+00BD> per cent over baseline in 2060 in the average country. In the LTM, new migrants are assumed
to have the same economic characteristics (employment rates, average productivity level) as the native-
born population, which could be an overoptimistic assumption in the short run even if justifiable in the
longer term. Thus, migration affects long-run fiscal gaps solely through changes in the age structure of the
population, with migrants assumed to be somewhat younger than the existing population. The calibration
is based on the average age profile of immigrants in European countries. The scenario can therefore be
interpreted as testing the sensitivity of long-run fiscal gaps to a younger age composition of the population.
Results show that increasing net migration by 0.1% of the population could reduce long-run fiscal gaps by
0.6 pp of GDP in the median country by 2060 (Figure 23). Most of this fiscal dividend comes through lower
pension expenditure relative to GDP, as higher migration reduces the ratio of retirees to workers. It also
lowers health and long-term care expenditure as a share of GDP (by reducing the proportion of the
population aged 65 and over) and in many cases increases other current primary expenditure slightly (by
increasing the ratio of youth to workers which is assumed to raise education spending as a share of GDP).

4. Complementary illustrations of the need for long-run fiscal adjustments

This section illustrates long-run fiscal adjustment needs within the LTM using two approaches that
complement the analysis in Section 2. Both confirm that, without policy action, many countries face
sizeable risks to long-run fiscal sustainability.

          Debt-to-GDP ratio trajectories

One way to highlight long-run fiscal challenges is to switch off the LTM<U+2019>s fiscal adjustment mechanism and
project the debt-to-GDP trajectory that results from holding structural primary revenue as a percentage of

directly into the fiscal balance and the debt trajectory. Because the approach assumes no policy response
to rising debt levels, some debt ratios begin to follow explosive paths. To avoid excessive feedback
between debt and interest rates via fiscal risk premia, the illustrative simulation ends in 2045.

5. The energy transition is potentially an additional source of expenditure
pressure

The energy transition, broader climate change mitigation efforts, and fiscal costs related to climate
adaptation and climate-related damages are additional sources of potential fiscal pressure that are only
partially included in the LTM and not included in the long-run fiscal gap indicator. This section updates and
refines the estimates of projected capital expenditure for renewable electricity generation first presented in
Guillemette and Ch<U+00E2>teau (2023[2]).
The LTM<U+2019>s baseline scenario is built on a business-as-usual premise, including for the relationship between
economic output and energy use and for the gradual transition from carbon-based to low-carbon energy
sources. The energy-substitution assumption is implemented by extrapolating country-level primary energy
mixes along time trends estimated over the past decade. This approach results in a projected pace of
substitution that is insufficient to achieve global net-zero greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions this century:
carbon-based sources still account for roughly three-quarters of the global primary energy mix in 2050 and
more than 60% in 2100.
The LTM can also be used to generate other stylised energy-transition scenarios with much more ambitious
substitution and efficiency gains. In the scenario used below, all countries accelerate decarbonisation,
phasing out coal by 2050 at the latest and reducing the shares of oil and gas in primary energy to 5% and
10%, respectively, by then. These declining shares are replaced by low-carbon sources, primarily solar
and wind, with the allocation varying by country according to stylised projection rules that reflect physical
constraints and revealed preferences, albeit imperfectly. 15 This scenario limits global warming to about

Capital investment in generation capacity is only one component of total investment needs. A renewable-
heavy grid also requires major investment in transmission lines, transformers, and other equipment, as

excludes many other necessary investments and does not capture differences in operating costs across
technologies.

The second term of [B2] is the rate of change in the ratio of the average pension to the average wage, also
called the average benefit ratio (not to be confused with the replacement rate, which usually refers to a

earnings in the benefit formulae of defined-benefit plans, the rate of indexation of benefits, the age structure
of the population of benefit recipients, rates of return on plan assets, etc. Projecting the future evolution of
benefit ratios thus requires a large amount of information, not just on current rules, but also on how they
will evolve given the many reforms that have already been legislated in many countries.

Under the simplifying assumption that the indexation rule for existing benefits alone drives the evolution of
the benefit ratio over time, this evolution can be connected to the projected trend labour productivity growth
rate. In countries where public pension benefits are indexed to the consumer price index, the average
benefit ratio would decline each year by the wedge between nominal wage growth and consumer price
inflation (i.e. real wage growth). In the LTM, real wage growth is equal to trend labour productivity growth
by construction, so with price indexation, the average benefit ratio falls at the rate of trend labour
productivity growth. In countries where public pension benefits are indexed to wages, the average benefit
ratio would remain constant, so the second term of [B2] would always be zero. Some countries use a
formula, or a series of discrete adjustments, to maintain the indexation of public pension benefits
somewhere between price indexation and full wage indexation.
