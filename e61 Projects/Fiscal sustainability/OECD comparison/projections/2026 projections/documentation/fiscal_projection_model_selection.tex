\begin{abstract}
This note is intended to make the model-selection judgement easy to challenge.  It begins with six reported top-down specifications for Australian general-government spending, writes out their mathematical structure, compares their projections and rolling forecast errors, and documents why the proposed ARDL/error-correction model is not accepted as a valid long-run model.  The top-down equations use a parsimonious demographic block containing the 0--14 and 65+ population shares.  Separate indicators for 2019--20, 2020--21 and 2021--22 absorb the COVID-affected observations.  The recommended top-down choice is the \emph{differenced ARIMAX family}, with the dynamic specification used for the principal projection after a concept-aligned PBO anchor comprising expenses plus net capital investment.  A mechanically differenced restricted version remains an internal nested benchmark, but is not presented as a separate result because it is not an independent model.  A COVID-adjusted ARIMA is retained as a near-term statistical benchmark.  The second part compares the baseline and data-enhanced bottom-up approaches.  The final part places the models together while preserving important differences in expenditure concepts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Top-down estimates and model selection}

\subsection{Decision in one paragraph}

The August 2026 PBO forecast of consolidated expenses plus net capital investment is used through 2029--30.  Beyond that point, the preferred \emph{driver-based top-down cross-check} is the differenced ARIMAX family, represented by its dynamic specification.  Among the reported models that can respond to demographic and economic drivers, it has the lowest five-year rolling RMSE (4.996 \pp\ of GDP) and the lowest maximum 2065--66 endpoint shift across the tested estimation windows (2.499 \pp).  The COVID-adjusted ARIMA has materially lower forecast errors, but its long-run path is essentially flat and cannot respond to ageing, relative government prices, unemployment, the terms of trade or policy.  It is therefore a near-term statistical benchmark, not the preferred structural cross-check.  The ARDL/ECM is rejected as the primary model because the required level relationship is not supported by the bounds tests, integration-order evidence is problematic, long-run coefficients remain collinear, and rolling forecast performance and endpoint stability are weak.

\subsection{Outcome, variables and common forecasting convention}

Let
\[
 y_t = \frac{G_t}{Y_t},
\]
where $G_t$ is the broad general-government expenditure concept constructed from ABS National Accounts and $Y_t$ is nominal GDP \cite{absna}.  Both are annual financial-year observations.  The estimation sample is 1979--80 to 2024--25 ($T=46$ before lags).

The common explanatory variables are
\begin{align*}
 \mathbf a_t &= (a^{0\text{--}14}_t,a^{65+}_t)',\\
 z^{\mathrm{tot}}_t &= \text{standardised terms-of-trade index},\\
 z^{\mathrm{rp}}_t &= \text{standardised relative government-consumption price index},\\
 u_t &= \text{unemployment rate},\\
 D_{j,t} &= \ind\{t=j\},\qquad j\in\{2019\text{--}20,2020\text{--}21,2021\text{--}22\}.
\end{align*}
The omitted demographic category is the aggregate 15--64 population share, so the two reported age coefficients are interpreted relative to that group.  The projection paths condition on common future paths for demographics and macroeconomic drivers.  ``Conditional'' is important: neither the forecast intervals nor rolling tests include uncertainty about those driver paths.

Every displayed long-run top-down path is joined in 2029--30 to a concept-aligned PBO broad-spending proxy: consolidated expenses plus net capital investment \cite{pbo,pbotech}.  The model supplies growth after the anchor rather than overwriting the official near-term forecast.  This recognises that the official forecast contains budget measures, current policy and institutional information not present in these parsimonious equations.

The National Accounts outcome used for estimation is constructed as general-government final consumption expenditure plus gross fixed capital formation plus total income payable, divided by nominal GDP.  PBO consolidated expenses are an operating-statement measure; the PBO reports net capital investment separately and stresses the distinction between recurrent and capital spending \cite{pbo,pbotech}.  Comparing the National Accounts outcome only with PBO expenses therefore omits the separately reported capital component.  In the latest PBO data pack, whose ratios are published to one decimal place, the 2024--25 National Accounts ratio is 38.341\% of GDP, PBO expenses are 36.6\%, and PBO net capital investment is 2.1\%.  Expenses plus net capital investment equal 38.7\%, which is 0.359 \pp\ above the National Accounts measure.

The raw 1.741 \pp\ difference between the National Accounts measure and PBO expenses in 2024--25 is therefore largely explained by capital investment.  Adding the full PBO net-capital item slightly over-corrects the gap.  The alignment is deliberately described as the closest available proxy rather than an exact accounting identity: the National Accounts variable contains \emph{gross} fixed capital formation, whereas the available PBO item is \emph{net} capital investment.  ABS documentation also identifies reconciliation adjustments for depreciation versus consumption of fixed capital, asset sales, FISIM and capitalised intangibles \cite{absgfsmethod,absgfsasna}.  The latest PBO vintage uses ABS GFS through 2024--25, incorporates local government in the national aggregates and uses 2026--27 budgets for 2025--26 to 2029--30; remaining differences can therefore reflect measurement concepts, consolidation, rounding, revisions and the GDP vintage rather than omitted capital alone \cite{pbotech}.

The PBO-anchored path does not estimate a bridge between these concepts.  For model $m$, it applies the additive splice
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde y^{(m)}_t=
 \begin{cases}
 y^{\mathrm{PBO,broad}}_t, & t\leq 2030,\\
 y^{\mathrm{PBO,broad}}_{2030}
 +\left(\widehat y^{(m)}_t-\widehat y^{(m)}_{2030}\right), & t>2030.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:pbo_splice}
\end{equation}
where $y^{\mathrm{PBO,broad}}_t$ is PBO expenses plus net capital investment.  Thus the concept-aligned PBO proxy determines the level through 2029--30 and the model determines only the subsequent change.  This treats the remaining join-year difference as an additive adjustment carried through the long run.  A separate model-only projection is reported below.  It starts from the latest National Accounts outturn and does not use the PBO spending path, although it retains the same future demographic and macroeconomic driver assumptions.

\subsection{Time periods and use of the historical data}
\label{sec:sample}

The raw National Accounts fiscal file runs from 1959--60 to 2024--25 (66 annual observations).  The top-down exercise does \emph{not} use the outcome-only observations before 1979--80.  The configuration sets a common estimation start of 1979--80 (labelled 1980 by financial-year end), and the model-ready dataset therefore contains 46 complete annual observations from 1979--80 to 2024--25.  There are no missing observations in the retained outcome, selected age shares, terms of trade, relative prices or unemployment.  All six reported top-down models use all observations in this common model-ready window, subject only to observations lost mechanically when first differences and lags are constructed.

The deliberate common window ensures that models are compared using the same span of outcome and driver data.  It should not be described as ``all ABS history'': the expenditure series itself is available back to 1959--60, but 1959--60 to 1978--79 is excluded by the explicit 1980 estimation-start choice.  Extending the common driver dataset and testing an earlier start would be a useful robustness exercise rather than something already embodied in the current estimates.

\subsection{The six reported specifications}

For the equations below, $L$ is the lag operator, $\Delta=1-L$, and $n_t$ denotes a stationary ARMA disturbance where relevant.  Automatic ARMA selection follows the approach of Hyndman and Khandakar \cite{hk} and is deliberately restricted to $p,q\leq2$ because the annual sample is short.

\subsubsection{1. Structural OLS in levels}

The transparent levels benchmark is
\begin{equation}
y_t=\alpha+\boldsymbol\beta_a'\mathbf a_t+
 \beta_{\mathrm{tot}}z^{\mathrm{tot}}_t+
 \beta_{\mathrm{rp}}z^{\mathrm{rp}}_t+
 \beta_u u_t+\sum_{j=2020}^{2022}\delta_jD_{j,t}+\varepsilon_t.
 \label{eq:ols}
\end{equation}
It is easy to interpret and is used for the Shapley attribution exercise.  It is not preferred as a standalone forecast because its residuals are autocorrelated (Ljung--Box $p=0.0012$) and its endpoint is sensitive to the estimation window.

\subsubsection{2. Levels ARIMAX}

The second model keeps the same level relationship but gives the residual a stationary ARMA process:
\begin{align}
 y_t &= \alpha+\boldsymbol\beta'\mathbf x_t+n_t,\\
 \phi(L)n_t&=\theta(L)\varepsilon_t,
 \label{eq:level_arimax}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf x_t=(\mathbf a_t',z^{\mathrm{tot}}_t,z^{\mathrm{rp}}_t,u_t,D_{2020,t},D_{2021,t},D_{2022,t})'$.  The selected error process is ARMA$(2,0)$.  It has a strong in-sample dynamic fit but weaker rolling forecasts and a 7.829 \pp\ maximum endpoint shift.

\subsubsection{3. Differenced ARIMAX family: dynamic specification (preferred driver-based cross-check)}

The preferred model retains a differenced outcome but gives each driver a transformation suited to its economic role:
\begin{align}
\Delta y_t={}&
 \boldsymbol\beta_a'\Delta\mathbf a_t
 +\beta_{q}\Delta z^{\mathrm{rp}}_t
 +\beta_{u0}\Delta u_t+\beta_{u1}\Delta u_{t-1} \\
&+\beta_{\tau0}\Delta z^{\mathrm{tot}}_t
 +\beta_{\tau1}\Delta z^{\mathrm{tot}}_{t-1}
 +\sum_{j=2020}^{2022}\delta_jD_{j,t}+n_t,\\
\phi(L)n_t={}&\theta(L)\varepsilon_t,
\label{eq:dynamic}
\end{align}
The three $D_{j,t}$ indicators absorb the annual spending changes in the COVID-affected financial years without imposing a common coefficient or an onset/unwind profile.  The selected residual is ARMA$(0,0)$.  Unemployment enters in changes: putting its level in a pure growth equation would add spending growth every year that unemployment remained high and create implausible drift.  Current and lagged terms-of-trade changes allow a budget or implementation delay.  Projected changes are accumulated from the observed endpoint, $\widehat y_{T+h}=y_T+\sum_{j=1}^{h}\widehat{\Delta y}_{T+j}$.

The code retains a mechanically differenced ARIMAX for internal benchmarking.  It uses the same selected age shares and the same three annual intervention indicators.  It is treated as part of the differenced-ARIMAX family rather than presented as a separate colleague-facing result.

\subsubsection{4. Hybrid structural/macro model}

The hybrid first estimates a slow-moving structural level,
\begin{equation}
 s_t=\alpha_s+\boldsymbol\beta_a'\mathbf a_t+\beta_q z^{\mathrm{rp}}_t
 +\sum_{j=2020}^{2022}\delta_jD_{j,t},
 \label{eq:hybrid_structural}
\end{equation}
then defines the unexplained annual change $r_t=\Delta y_t-\Delta\widehat s_t$ and estimates
\begin{align}
r_t={}&\gamma_{u0}\Delta u_t+\gamma_{u1}\Delta u_{t-1}
+\gamma_{\tau0}\Delta z^{\mathrm{tot}}_t
+\gamma_{\tau1}\Delta z^{\mathrm{tot}}_{t-1}+n_t.
\label{eq:hybrid_macro}
\end{align}
The annual indicators are included in the structural level equation.  Their fitted first differences therefore absorb the pandemic-year movements when $r_t$ is constructed, and they are not repeated in the macro residual equation.  The forecast recombines $\Delta\widehat s_t$ and $\widehat r_t$ and accumulates them.  Its 2065--66 endpoint changes by up to 6.647 \pp\ across estimation windows, so it remains a secondary structural cross-check.

\subsubsection{5. ARDL-style unrestricted ECM}

The fitted error-correction equation is
\begin{align}
\Delta y_t={}&\alpha+\lambda y_{t-1}
+\boldsymbol\theta_a'\mathbf a_{t-1}
+\theta_q z^{\mathrm{rp}}_{t-1}
+\delta_u u_{t-1}\\
&+\gamma_q\Delta z^{\mathrm{rp}}_t
+\gamma_u\Delta u_t
+\gamma_{\tau0}\Delta z^{\mathrm{tot}}_t
+\gamma_{\tau1}\Delta z^{\mathrm{tot}}_{t-1}\\
&+\rho\Delta y_{t-1}+\sum_{j=2020}^{2022}\delta_jD_{j,t}+\varepsilon_t.
\label{eq:ecm}
\end{align}
The fitted adjustment coefficient is $\widehat\lambda=-0.238$.  If a valid level relationship existed, the implied long-run coefficients would be $-\widehat\theta_j/\widehat\lambda$.  A negative $\widehat\lambda$ is necessary for convergence but is \emph{not} evidence of cointegration.  The non-standard bounds tests and integration-order requirements must also be satisfied; Section~\ref{sec:ardl_rejection} shows that they are not.

\subsubsection{6. ARIMA benchmark with COVID controls}

The statistical benchmark is an ARIMA with the three annual interventions,
\begin{equation}
 \Phi(L)(1-L)^d\left(y_t-\sum_{j=2020}^{2022}\delta_jD_{j,t}\right)
 =c+\Theta(L)\varepsilon_t.
 \label{eq:arima}
\end{equation}
Automatic selection chooses ARIMA$(1,0,2)$.  It has the best rolling accuracy, but its anchored long-run forecast is essentially flat at 36.808\% of GDP.  That property is useful as a ``no-driver'' benchmark and unsuitable as the main long-run model.

\subsection{Rolling forecast evaluation}

For each origin $o=2010,\ldots,2020$, each model is re-estimated using only data available through $o$.  Forecasts are produced for horizons $h=1,\ldots,5$ using the subsequently observed driver paths.  Thus this is a test of the spending equation conditional on drivers, not a fully real-time forecast exercise.  For model $m$,
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{RMSE}_{m,h}
 =100\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_h}\sum_o
 \left(\widehat y^{(m)}_{o+h\mid o}-y_{o+h}\right)^2}.
 \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
There are 11 forecast origins at each reported horizon.  COVID observations are retained and handled by the intervention variables where applicable.

\subsection{Why the ARDL/ECM is rejected}
\label{sec:ardl_rejection}

The ECM is not rejected because its projected path is high, nor because the adjustment coefficient has an inconvenient sign.  It is rejected as the preferred specification because the conditions required to interpret equation~\eqref{eq:ecm} as an error-correction model are not established.

\subsubsection*{1. Integration order is not cleanly admissible}

ARDL bounds methods allow a mixture of $I(0)$ and $I(1)$ regressors, but not $I(2)$ variables \cite{pss}.  ADF and KPSS tests support first-difference stationarity for spending/GDP, relative government prices and unemployment.  The result for the 0--14 share is inconclusive, while the 65+ share produces an $I(2)$ warning because first-difference stationarity is not established.  These tests have low power against smooth demographic trends, but that uncertainty weakens rather than strengthens the case for bounds inference.

\subsubsection*{2. The fitted level relationship fails both bounds tests}

Finite-sample critical values were simulated using 20,000 replications for Case III (unrestricted intercept, no trend) and four long-run regressors: the two age shares, relative government prices and unemployment.

\subsubsection*{4. Long-run coefficients are weakly identified}

Age shares move together and sum to one.  With the aggregate 15--64 share omitted, the maximum VIF is 19.7 and the standardised design condition number is 10.6.  Individual implied long-run age effects remain too collinear to support a strong structural interpretation.

\subsubsection*{5. Forecast performance and sample sensitivity do not rescue the model}

The ECM has the highest five-year rolling RMSE of the six reported models and its 2065--66 endpoint changes by 11.650 \pp\ when the estimation window changes.  Its 43.29\% endpoint is retained in the ensemble as a sensitivity, but there is no statistical basis for labelling that path the estimated long-run equilibrium.

\subsection{Selection conclusion and proposed use}

The proposed hierarchy is:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item use the August 2026 PBO consolidated expenses plus net capital investment through 2029--30 as the closest available bridge to the broad top-down outcome;
\item describe the preferred top-down approach as the differenced ARIMAX family and use its dynamic specification as the principal projection;
\item retain the ARIMA with COVID controls as the near-term statistical benchmark;
\item show the complete top-down ensemble as specification uncertainty;
\item retain the ECM as an experimental sensitivity, but do not use its implied long-run coefficients or endpoint as the central estimate.
\end{enumerate}

This is a choice of the best current cross-check, not a claim that the dynamic specification is a forty-year causal model.  Its forecast intervals widen substantially because annual changes are accumulated, it remains conditional on assumed driver paths, and its endpoint varies across estimation windows.  The estimation-through-2019 check is within 0.85 \pp\ of the full-sample endpoint, while the 1989--90 start shifts it by 2.50 \pp.

\section{Comparison of the two bottom-up approaches}

\subsection{Baseline: fixed demographic exposures and calibrated excess costs}

Let $S_{c,t}$ denote nominal primary spending in category $c$, $E_{c,t}$ its demographic exposure, $\pi_t$ inflation and $g^{\mathrm{prod}}_t$ the common real-income/productivity allowance.  For non-defence categories,
\begin{align}
S_{c,t}&=S_{c,t-1}
\left(\frac{E_{c,t}}{E_{c,t-1}}\right)
\left(1+\pi_t+g^{\mathrm{prod}}_t+\kappa_{c,s}\right),
\label{eq:bottom_base}\\
g^{\mathrm{prod}}_t&=\max\{g^{\mathrm{nomGDP}}_t-\pi_t-g^{\mathrm{pop}}_t,0\}.
\end{align}
Here $\kappa_{c,s}$ is the category excess-cost rate in scenario $s$.  Exposure is total population or a fixed age-weighted population for health, education and social protection, using Centre for Population projections \cite{population}.  The base category levels are from ABS GFS \cite{absgfs}.  The central excess-cost assumptions are 0.75\% for health; 0.25\% for education, public order and social protection; and zero elsewhere.  These are transparent modelling judgements, not published estimates.  The exposure--price--intensity decomposition follows the broad logic used in Australian long-run fiscal projections \cite{igr}.

Defence is exogenous:
\begin{equation}
 S_{\mathrm{def},t}=Y_t d_{s,t},
\end{equation}
where $d_{s,t}$ transitions from the observed share to a scenario target by 2034--35.  Category totals are scaled to the official primary-spending path through 2029--30; the final scale factor is then held fixed.

\subsection{Data-enhanced sensitivity}

The second approach keeps the same demographic exposures and defence rule, but uses online ABS public-sector Wage Price Index (WPI) series \cite{abswpi} and the recent GFS purpose history.  For category $c$,
\begin{align}
e_{c,t}&=g(S_{c,t})-g(E_{c,t})-g(W^{\mathrm{all}}_t),\\
p_{c,t}&=g(W^c_t)-g(W^{\mathrm{all}}_t),\\
i_{c,t}&=g(S_{c,t})-g(E_{c,t})-g(W^c_t),\\
e_{c,t}&=p_{c,t}+i_{c,t},
\label{eq:enhanced_decomp}
\end{align}
where $g(x_t)=x_t/x_{t-1}-1$.  The enhanced central rate is
\begin{equation}
 \kappa^{\mathrm{enh}}_c
 =0.65\kappa^{\mathrm{base}}_c
 +0.35\,\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
 \operatorname{median}_{t\in\mathcal T}e_{c,t},-0.02,0.02\right),
 \label{eq:shrink}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal T$ contains six non-COVID annual changes.  Half of the departure from baseline is damped away by 2039--40.  The 35\% weight, cap and damping are conservative modelling choices reflecting the short, break-prone ten-year GFS panel.

\section{Placing all specifications together}

\subsection{What can and cannot be compared directly}

The top-down outcome is a broad National Accounts expenditure ratio with interest embedded.  The bottom-up category model projects primary spending and then adds endogenous debt interest in the debt module.  These concepts should not be silently overlaid.  Figure~\ref{fig:all_models} uses the baseline bottom-up \emph{total} expense ratio after adding endogenous interest, which is the closest workflow comparison with the top-down outcome.  The enhanced bottom-up result is currently a primary-spending sensitivity; its debt and interest feedback has not been rerun, so it is shown separately in Table~\ref{tab:all_endpoints} rather than as a directly comparable total-expense line.

The official forecast handles the near term; the bottom-up baseline is the central long-run fiscal-sustainability framework; the differenced ARIMAX family, represented by its dynamic specification, is the preferred driver-based aggregate cross-check; the COVID-adjusted ARIMA is the near-term statistical benchmark; and the remaining equations and bottom-up enhanced version define transparent specification sensitivities.

\section{Questions for internal review}

Colleagues can most usefully sense-check the following points:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item Is PBO expenses plus net capital investment the right bridge for the broad National Accounts outcome, and should the remaining historical reconciliation be estimated explicitly?
\item Are the two selected demographic shares and the variable-specific transformations in equation~\eqref{eq:dynamic} economically appropriate, particularly the use of changes in unemployment and relative prices?
\item Should rolling forecasts be supplemented by genuinely real-time driver vintages, rather than conditioning on subsequently observed drivers?
\item Are 2010--2020 origins representative enough, and are separate FY2020--22 interventions sufficient to isolate the pandemic observations?
\item Is the 2029--30 PBO anchor the correct join point, and should model growth be blended gradually rather than joined immediately?
\item Does the ARDL rejection logic omit any credible alternative lag order, deterministic case or smaller long-run block worth testing?
\item For the bottom-up model, are the fixed age weights and baseline excess-cost assumptions reasonable, and which category should receive the next service-volume module?
\item Should the enhanced bottom-up primary path be passed through the full debt-interest recursion before it is included in the combined comparison?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reproducibility and limitations}

All results in this note are generated from the project tables and scripts.  Key files are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \model{R/model\_functions\_2.R}: mathematical implementation of the six reported top-down specifications and the internal restricted differenced-ARIMAX benchmark;
\item \model{scripts/30\_forecast\_checks.R}: rolling-origin forecasts;
\item \model{scripts/31\_model\_diagnostics.R}: fit, interval and sensitivity diagnostics;
\item \model{scripts/32\_ecm\_validation.R}: integration, finite-sample bounds, specification, stability and collinearity tests;
\item \model{scripts/10\_bottom\_up\_projection.R}: baseline bottom-up method;
\item \model{scripts/11\_bottom\_up\_data\_enhanced.R}: online-price/data-enhanced sensitivity;
\item \model{outputs/tables/model\_selection\_assessment.csv}: consolidated top-down selection evidence;
\item \model{outputs/tables/top\_down\_anchor\_comparison.csv}: National Accounts, PBO and model-only level reconciliation;
\item \model{outputs/tables/ecm\_validation\_summary.csv}: ARDL/ECM decision evidence.
\end{itemize}

The exercise remains conditional, not causal.  Model spread is not a probability interval; conditional forecast intervals omit driver and policy uncertainty; structural breaks are difficult to identify in a 46-year annual sample; and the bottom-up excess-cost estimates use only a ten-year consistent GFS purpose panel.  These limitations motivate triangulation rather than false precision.
