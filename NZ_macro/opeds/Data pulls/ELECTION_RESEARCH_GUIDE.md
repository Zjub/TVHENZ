# Election economics: evidence and reading paths

**Ageing extension:** [28 additional charts](figures/ageing/index.html) and an [ageing research guide](AGEING_RESEARCH_GUIDE.md) investigate historical spending, population structure, health-demand benchmarks and long-term fiscal projections.

Snapshot: **2 November 2026**. This is research for articles and companion blog posts, not drafted op-eds or a preferred conclusion.

Start with the [new searchable gallery](figures/election_2026/index.html). It contains **63 charts**, each with PNG, SVG, plotted CSV, source links and interpretation notes. The [earlier 70-chart pack](figures/op_ed_topics/index.html) remains available, including the longer benefit histories and public-service measures.

The six election areas are retained. The inflation/CGT area is developed as a coherent **non-taxation of inflation** investigation. A seventh section adds **compulsory retirement saving versus pre-funding an annuity**, covering both individual annuitisation and collective public funding.

The [numeric research notes](processed/election_2026/key_findings.csv) provide values, units, periods, observation/model status and caveats for writing articles that cannot contain charts. A model illustration must still be described as an illustration when used in prose.

## 1. Fiscal promises: what is doing the work?

**Reading path:** F03 → F01 → F04 → F05 → F02 → F06.

The observed/forecast distinction matters immediately. PREFU's published tax-to-GDP ratio rises from **27.6% in 2025/26** to **29.1% in 2030/31**; expenses fall from **32.1% to 29.8%**. These are the baseline assumptions against which new promises should be assessed, not results delivered by unimplemented election policies. The underlying workbooks are [Treasury PREFU 2026](https://www.treasury.govt.nz/publications/efu/pre-election-economic-and-fiscal-update-2026).

F03 shows why a May Budget costing and a September costing can differ even without a new policy. F04 decomposes the change in **core Crown revenue minus expenses**, explicitly not consolidated OBEGALx. F02 then shows why an operating surplus does not immediately mean positive residual cash. F06 prevents comparisons using different debt definitions.

**Questions to test:** How much fiscal improvement depends on rising tax receipts? Which future operating allowances remain unallocated? Does an announced saving mean an annual flow, a four-year total, or a reduction against an already-growing forecast?

**What could change the interpretation:** Higher receipts from sustainable real growth differ from receipts caused by inflation. A restraint path may be achievable through productivity or require service changes. Neither headcounts nor nominal spending alone answers that question.

**Missing measurement:** An independently reconciled, common-baseline ledger of every party's proposals; service-specific demand and input-price adjustments; distributional microsimulation. The charts do **not** present a complete party scorecard or claim a causal fiscal-drag revenue estimate. Treasury's explanation of fiscal drag also includes progression as nominal incomes grow; it cannot all be labelled a tax on inflation.

## 2. Non-taxation of inflation: define income consistently

**Reading path:** N08 → N01 → N02 → N03 → N05 → N06 → N07. N04 checks tax-rate sensitivity.

Three mechanisms need separate treatment:

1. **Progressive income-tax thresholds.** A worker's income can rise with prices while their real earnings stay fixed. N01 holds real earnings and tax rates constant and compares frozen with indexed thresholds. At $70,000 starting real earnings and 2% inflation, the ten-year endpoint difference is about **$1,576 more annual tax in starting-year dollars**. It is a household scenario, not measured nationwide revenue. The scale comes from [IRD's current personal rates](https://www.ird.govt.nz/income-tax/income-tax-for-individuals/tax-codes-and-tax-rates-for-individuals/tax-rates-for-individuals).
2. **Interest income.** N03 distinguishes the inflation compensation in nominal interest from the real return. N05 shows compounding. The assumed nominal rate is 5%, not an observed current deposit quote. The indexed comparator taxes positive nominal interest net of inflation compensation and does not refund negative real returns.
3. **Capital gains.** N06–N07 compare nominal and real gains under the proposed 28% property CGT. The historical detailed design explicitly excludes inflation indexation; the [current policy page](https://www.labour.org.nz/capitalgainstax) and [original detailed document](https://www.interest.co.nz/sites/default/files/2025-10/Labour%20CGT-oct25.pdf) are both saved. A $500,000 eligible property rising only with 2% inflation for ten years produces about **$25,151 tax in starting-year purchasing power** under this simple illustration. It starts at the proposed valuation/acquisition date, not the property's original historical purchase date.

**Useful distinction for writing:** Removing taxation of inflation is a principle about defining real income. It does not by itself establish the appropriate tax rate, exempt asset boundary or case for/against CGT. Nor does it mean indexing house-price gains to a house-price index: the counterfactual here uses general consumer-price inflation.

**What could change the interpretation:** Revenue replacement, administrative costs, asymmetry between assets, interest deductions for borrowers, capital improvements, realised losses and valuation rules. Comprehensive indexation should examine liabilities as well as assets; this pack's saving model does not resolve that symmetry. At zero real gain an effective rate expressed as tax divided by real gain is undefined; N07 omits those cases rather than reporting a misleading percentage.

**Missing measurement:** Taxpayer-level income/asset data, actual holding-period and cost distributions, aggregate fiscal cost, behavioural responses. Do not sum the household scenarios into an implied fiscal estimate or describe the scenarios as forecasts of property returns.

## 3. Benefits: which spending could a proposal affect?

**Reading path:** W06 → W01 → W03 → W04 → W02 → W05; then the earlier B11–B29 histories.

PREFU's 2025/26 unaudited figures put **NZ Super at about 53.5% of welfare benefit expenses**. The whole **Jobseeker plus Emergency Benefit programme is about 3.37% of core Crown expenses**. That second number includes health-related recipients and is an upper spending base for many narrower proposals, not an estimate of work-ready unemployment spending.

W02 identifies which benefits drive forecast expense growth. W03 distinguishes work-ready and health-condition/disability categories; W04 supplies actual labour-market context. W05 then makes replacement assistance and lost-tax offsets explicit instead of presenting gross expense reductions as net savings. See the [PREFU expense tables](https://www.treasury.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2026-09/prefu26-data-expense-tables.xlsx) and [benefit history definitions](BENEFIT_HISTORY.md).

**Questions to test:** Is the proposal a rate cut, a sanction, a change in eligibility, or assistance into employment? How many people can actually be affected? Are savings temporary or durable? What happens to household incomes and other assistance?

**What could change the interpretation:** A policy that improves durable employment can affect earnings, taxes and wellbeing beyond the benefit expense line. A small fiscal saving could still have large household effects. Conversely, a large gross reduction need not be a large net saving. The data should determine the scale for each policy.

**Missing measurement:** Work-ready payment flows, longitudinal transitions, replacement payments, administration costs and credible policy impact estimates. Administrative recipient stocks are not HLFS unemployment or a measure of willingness to work.

## 4. Health: access is an economic output

**Reading path:** H01 → H02 → H03 → H04 → H05 → H06 → H08; use H07 for fiscal context.

The 2024/25 survey estimates that **25.5% of adults faced a GP waiting-time barrier**, compared with **14.9% facing a cost barrier**. These are overlapping experiences, not categories to add or a count of consultations. H01 supplies history; H02–H05 show differences across age, deprivation, disability and ethnicity with published 95% confidence intervals. The complete [Ministry of Health CSV](https://minhealthnz.shinyapps.io/nz-health-survey-2024-25-annual-data-explorer/data/nz-health-survey-2024-25-prevalences.csv) is downloaded automatically.

**Questions to test:** How much spending removes a charge on an existing visit, and how much creates timely additional care? Which groups gain? Does the workforce, premises, administrative time or appointment system constrain delivery?

H08 is a sensitivity calculation, **not** an estimate that a subsidy cannot work. [Labour's Medicard proposal](https://www.labour.org.nz/medicard) includes supply measures as well as free visits; evaluating it requires evidence on those measures, not just counting subsidised appointments. H07 shows existing health baselines, with an explicit warning that future operating allowances have not all been allocated to services.

**What could change the interpretation:** Earlier access may prevent costly hospital care and improve health; that benefit can exceed the number of appointments. Extra visits can also displace other clinical work. Survey barriers do not quantify these effects.

**Missing measurement:** Consistently defined clinical FTEs, appointment durations, practice capacity, additional completed visits and outcomes per dollar. This pack does not yet provide a verified historical GP clinical-FTE series or estimate GP productivity. Neighbourhood deprivation is not household income; ethnic groups overlap; differences shown are unadjusted.

## 5. Recovery: prices, earnings and household circumstances

**Reading path:** L01 → L02 → L03 → L04 → L06 → L07. L05 is a sensitivity comparison, not measured group income.

September 2026 annual living-cost inflation was **3.0% for all households**, **4.0% for superannuitant households**, **3.4% for the lowest-income quintile** and **2.7% for the highest-income quintile**. L02 shows cumulative changes, which are a different question from whether annual inflation has fallen. [Stats NZ's September release](https://www.stats.govt.nz/information-releases/household-living-costs-price-indexes-september-2026-quarter/) explains why HLPIs, which include interest, differ from CPI.

L04 distinguishes average hourly earnings from the fixed-quality labour cost index and deflates both with CPI. L06 adds employment and hours context. L07 shows why groups' exposure to mortgage interest and rent differs. These observations support a more careful question about recovery than an aggregate GDP growth number alone.

**What could change the interpretation:** Wage growth, employment, paid hours, taxes, transfers, mortgage repricing and income from assets can move in different directions. A national hourly wage index deflated by retirees' costs is **not** retirees' observed income growth; L05 deliberately labels this as a benchmark comparison.

**Missing measurement:** Current disposable income after housing costs by tenure, age and income group, with comparable household definitions. The pack does not claim to have measured complete household income recovery. QES, LCI and HLFS cover different concepts and cannot simply be multiplied into a representative household's income.

## 6. Business tax: measure the investment margin

**Reading path:** C01 → C02 → C03 → C04 → C05 → C07 → C08, with C06 for international context. Earlier T01–T14 cover screen/game spending, disclosure and R&D detail.

C01–C05 compare a lower company rate, standard depreciation, Investment Boost and full expensing for a common hypothetical asset. A lower tax rate benefits profits beyond new investment; faster deductions affect the timing of the tax shield. A firm without taxable profit may not receive immediate cash from a deduction. These differences should be visible before claiming any option is the best growth policy.

The 20% SME rate is [NZ First's proposal](https://www.nzfirst.nz/nzfirst_to_campaign_on_20_tax_rate_for_smes), not enacted policy. Current [Investment Boost](https://www.ird.govt.nz/investment-boost) allows 20% up front with depreciation on the remaining 80%. The asset's 20% diminishing-value depreciation rate is an **assumption**, not a universal statutory rate.

C05 has zero inflation, sufficient profits, no leverage, no resale value and a perpetual cash-flow investment. Under those assumptions full expensing gives a zero effective marginal wedge. That is a model result, not measured evidence that expensing has no fiscal cost or that every tax on capital disappears. Imputation and shareholder taxes are omitted.

**What could change the interpretation:** Additional investment induced per dollar of support, losses, financing, tax arbitrage, transition benefits for existing assets and compliance costs. For a turnover threshold, check the proposed transition rules before asserting that a particular cliff exists.

**Missing measurement:** Counterfactual R&D/investment without support, linked firm tax/turnover/profit data, subsidy incidence and the detailed SME policy design. C07 spending and C08 credit expense cannot identify R&D additionality; their ratio is not a return on public spending. Unestimated tax expenditures are not zero and published tax expenditure estimates cannot all be added as repeal savings.

## 7. Compulsory saving versus pre-funding an annuity

**Reading path:** S01 → S02 → S05 → S06 → S07 → S08 → S10 → S11 → S14 → S15 → S20. S16 addresses who pays; S18 checks pension indexation; S19 anchors the public pension baseline.

The central conceptual question is that **compulsion, funding, ownership and benefit form are different choices**:

| Design dimension | Choices that should be stated |
|---|---|
| Participation | Voluntary, automatic enrolment with opt-out, compulsory |
| Ownership | Individual account or collective assets supporting a promise |
| Financing | Current revenue, partial pre-funding, full pre-funding |
| Retirement benefit | Accessible balance, fixed drawdown, lifetime annuity |
| Benefit allocation | Earnings-related, flat pension, targeted pension or hybrid |
| Risk | Who bears market, longevity, inflation, fiscal and political risks? |

An individually owned account can buy a lifetime annuity. A public lifelong pension can be paid from current taxes or partly backed by a fund. The NZ Super Fund is already collective pre-funding, but a chart of its returns is not evidence that the whole NZ Super promise is funded. [National's compulsory KiwiSaver proposal](https://www.national.org.nz/news/260621-national-to-further-boost-kiwis-financia) should be distinguished from the [current IRD rules](https://www.ird.govt.nz/kiwisaver-changes).

**What the observed data establish:** IRD records substantial membership without annual contributions, but that includes children, retirees, people overseas and some incomplete direct-provider contribution reporting. It cannot be presented as the proportion of workers refusing to save. S03 covers flows through IRD, not all provider contributions; S04 records selected early withdrawals. First-home withdrawals can transfer wealth into housing, rather than destroy it.

S05–S08 use [Stats NZ's complete 2022–2024 period life tables](https://www.stats.govt.nz/information-releases/national-and-subnational-period-life-tables-2022-2024/) to distinguish a balance from insurance against outliving it. The illustrations price a single-life pension of **$30,000 per year in constant CPI purchasing power**, beginning at age 65. With a 2% real liability discount and 5% assumed loading, the equal-weight male/female model requires about **$516,000 at retirement**. This is neither a market annuity quote nor a valuation of statutory NZ Super.

**What the comparisons test:**

- S08 isolates longevity pooling against funding everyone to a fixed age. Those contracts differ in bequests and guarantees; a lower actuarial price is not a free welfare improvement for every person.
- S09–S13 translate net deposits into balances and then income. Forty years of 8% net invested contributions from constant $70,000 real earnings produces about $422,000 at a 3% net real return, supporting about $24,600 on the model's annuity basis. Headline employee/employer percentages are not net deposits after ESCT.
- S11 shows how earlier and later career interruptions differ. It does not estimate actual group gaps or include caregiver credits.
- S12 isolates fees. Higher returns are not assigned to a higher liability discount rate: investment risk does not mechanically make a promised income cheaper to guarantee.
- S14 holds total deposits fixed in an artificial equal-sized earnings grid and compares an earnings-related annuity with a common pension. Redistribution is explicit; the model does not claim one ownership structure creates extra resources.
- S15 shows the resource requirement during transition. Existing pensions still need to be paid. Borrowing to buy fund assets increases gross assets without an equal increase in net public wealth.
- S16 compares fixed gross salary with fixed employer labour cost. It tests incidence assumptions rather than predicting that employer contributions are entirely paid by workers.
- S18 distinguishes CPI-level payments from a growing real pension. NZ Super's statutory wage/CPI protections are not represented by a fixed CPI-level target.
- S20 makes displaced saving and public dissaving explicit. A bigger mandatory account balance is not, by itself, an equal increase in national saving.

**What could change the interpretation:** A credible low-cost pool with good risk sharing could support better income security. Individual ownership may provide flexibility and bequests people value. Differences in survival by income and ethnicity can alter the distribution of a flat contribution/annuity design. Funding changes also affect generations differently, particularly those paying existing pensions while building new assets.

**Missing measurement:** Additional national saving caused by compulsion, actual annuity prices and demand, cohort mortality improvement, heterogeneous survival, household preferences, transition tax bases, guarantees and who bears losses. This pack does not price joint-life annuities, refunds, capital guarantees or the whole national pension system. Account withdrawals and taxable public pensions also have different tax treatment; a proper replacement design must match **after-tax income**, not compare an account payout with a gross NZ Super rate.

## Model and reproducibility notes

Run from `opeds` in PowerShell:

```powershell
& "C:\Program Files\R\R-4.5.2\bin\Rscript.exe" "Data pulls\10_run_election_pack.R"
```

Reproduce saved snapshots or only regenerate figures:

```powershell
& "C:\Program Files\R\R-4.5.2\bin\Rscript.exe" "Data pulls\10_run_election_pack.R" --cached
& "C:\Program Files\R\R-4.5.2\bin\Rscript.exe" "Data pulls\10_run_election_pack.R" --plots-only
```

The earlier `05_run_topic_pack.R` must have been run once: this extension reuses its historical benefit, R&D and Budget data without overwriting that pack. Files `08_pull_election_data.R`, `09_make_election_plots.R` and `10_run_election_pack.R` provide the separate extension. Packages are checked, never installed.

Downloads are pinned to releases, not automatically discovered on every run. Live IRD and NZSF URLs can change; `--cached` preserves the saved snapshot. The manifest records URLs, hashes, timestamps and reuse. A small standard-library Python helper is used only if Windows R encounters a TLS handshake error; certificate verification stays enabled. Set `NZ_OPED_PYTHON` if Python is not on PATH.

Assumptions are exported to `processed/election_2026/parameters.csv` for audit. It is an output, not a configuration input. To change scenarios, edit the model constants/grids in `08` and their captions in `09` together, then rerun. No empirical probabilities are attached to the 1%, 3% and 5% return scenarios.

The main annuity calculation is approximately:

`capital = annual income × sum[(Lx / l65) / (1 + real discount)^(age − 65 + payment timing)] × (1 + loading)`

`Lx` is expected person-years alive in the age interval; `l65` is survivors at 65. The full 105+ open interval is included, with approximate mean payment timing based on its remaining life expectancy. Median life-table estimates are used; mortality credible intervals are retained in the full download but not propagated through the model. At zero discount the factor is checked against published remaining life expectancy. Equal-weight pooling means equal numbers of male and female survivors at 65, not actual buyer/population weights. Period mortality is held fixed, so these are not cohort forecasts.

Accumulation uses annual end-year deposits, a constant real salary and returns net of fees/tax. There are no pre-retirement death credits. The target payout is a constant purchasing-power income with no extra payout tax imposed in the illustration; any comparison with a taxable public benefit needs explicit gross/net matching. The 5% annuity loading is assumed, not observed. Contributions, administrative cost, safety buffers and risk guarantees must be financed somewhere in any complete public design.

The source checks reconcile PREFU benefit components, date headers, CPI coverage, HLFS/health anchors, life-table monotonicity, annuity factors and lifetime deductions. Every plotted dataset is exported. Figures remain descriptive or model-based unless explicitly labelled otherwise; none identify a causal election-policy effect.
