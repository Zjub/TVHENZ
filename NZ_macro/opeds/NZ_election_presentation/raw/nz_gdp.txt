Gross domestic product (GDP) provides a snapshot of the performance of the economy. GDP is New Zealand’s official measure of economic growth.

New Zealand’s real purchasing power falls in the June 2026 quarter In the June 2026 quarter, compared with the March 2026 quarter: real gross national disposable income fell 0.4 percent real gross national disposable income per capita fell 0.6 percent. In the year ended June 2026, compared with the year ended June 2025: annual real gross national disposable income rose 2.5 percent annual real gross national disposable income per capita rose 1.9 percent. While GDP measures economic activity, real gross national disposable income (RGNDI) measures the volumes of goods and services that New Zealand residents have command over, that is, the real purchasing power of the country’s disposable income. RGNDI is also affected by changes in the terms of trade, the country’s net investment income, and net transfer flows with the rest of the world. New Zealand’s ability to buy goods and services from its income, RGNDI, fell 0.4 percent in the June 2026 quarter. Net transfer flows increased, terms of trade decreased, and net investment income on our international investments increased. Over the June 2026 quarter, export prices rose 3.5 percent, while import prices rose 13.8 percent, resulting in a decrease in the terms of trade. A decrease in the terms of trade results in more exports being needed to pay for a given volume of imports. This means that residents can purchase fewer goods and services by volume from the income generated from a given level of domestic production. The 0.4 percent decrease in RGNDI, coupled with a population increase of 0.2 percent over the quarter, decreased RGNDI per capita by 0.6 percent in the quarter. Annually, RGNDI rose 2.5 percent over the year to June 2026 while RGNDI per capita rose 1.9 percent over the year. Impact of conflict in the Middle East on GDP in the June 2026 quarter The measures of GDP in the June 2026 quarter were impacted by the Middle East conflict and related fuel price increases. Our headline measure of GDP is presented in volume terms, which removes the effect of price changes. However, GDP for some industries related to fuel saw changes which could be attributed to the large increases in fuel prices during the quarter. In retail trade and accommodation, fuel retailing fell in volume terms in the June 2026 quarter and was the largest quarterly decline since the COVID-19-affected September 2021 quarter. Accommodation and food services fell 3.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter, indicating a potential change in discretionary spending. In household consumption expenditure (HCE), the restaurants and hotels group fell in the June 2026 quarter, indicating a similar change in discretionary spending to that seen in the accommodation and food services sub-industry on the production side. Transport, postal, and warehousing fell 1.7 percent in the June 2026 quarter, driven by a decline in road transport. The quarterly method for this sub-industry has used diesel delivery volumes since the March 2022 quarter. The transport group of HCE fell 1.6 percent in the June 2026 quarter. The petrol and other vehicle fuels and lubricants components of non-durable goods HCE also fell during the June 2026 quarter. Imports of refined petroleum in constant prices rose 5.3 percent in the June 2026 quarter compared with the March 2026 quarter, but rose 63.8 percent in current prices over the same period. Imports of intermediate goods, processed fuels and lubricants (which includes diesel) in constant prices rose 19.8 percent in the June 2026 quarter compared with the March 2026 quarter, but rose 123.8 percent in current prices over the same period. Increases in quarterly prices for petrol imports were the highest since December 1990, while increases for processed fuels and lubricants were the highest on record (data from September 1990). We made no methodological changes to quarterly GDP in response to the Middle East conflict and elevated fuel prices.
