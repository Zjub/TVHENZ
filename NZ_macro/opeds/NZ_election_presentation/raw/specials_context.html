Special votes

How the official count could change the House and the closest electorates after the 2026 election

Who has the numbers

On election night National, ACT and NZ First had 67 of 131 seats, 1 more than the 66 a majority needs, and Labour, the Greens, Te Pāti Māori and Opportunity had 64. About 399,365 special votes are still to be counted, 96,947 of them from overseas. They change each party’s share of the party vote, which sets how many seats it is entitled to, and they decide the closest electorates. This section simulates both together and counts how often each side ends with a majority.

How the majority could change

The Government’s margin over every other party was 3 seats on election night. A list seat lost by a governing party that has list seats moves the margin by 2, because another party gains the seat. National holds more electorates than its party vote entitles it to, so its changes count once: when its entitlement falls it keeps its electorates and another party gains a seat, and when it loses an electorate nobody gains one. A change of 4 gives the other parties a one-seat majority; 3 is a tie. There are 14 ways to make exactly 4:

A count can also reach that one-seat majority with offsetting changes (National gaining an electorate while losing more elsewhere, or Te Pāti Māori losing one), which takes the chance of exactly a one-seat majority to 15%. A bigger majority for the other parties has a chance of 23%. Together these are the 37% above, so the routes that go further are already counted in it.

Wellington Bays: Labour against the Greens

On election night Craig Renney (Labour) led Julie Anne Genter (Green) by 140 votes in Wellington Bays, of 40,699 counted. The model above is fitted to National against Labour, so this contest has its own: the same model, fitted to the official results of Auckland Central, Wellington Bays (notional) and Wellington Central (rest) at the 2017, 2020 and 2023 elections (9 seat-elections), with the Labour candidate in place of National’s. It shares each simulation’s national special-vote count and party-vote lean with the rest of the page. The winner does not change the House: the Labour and Green seat totals are set by their party votes, so the seat decides who sits, not how many. Wellington Bays is new in 2026. Its past results here are notional, all of Rongotai's votes and those cast at Wellington Central's voting places in Brooklyn and Mount Cook, which is where the boundary review took it from. People can vote anywhere, so voting places only approximate the area. Julie Anne Genter won Rongotai from Labour in 2023. On those boundaries the 2023 result would have been Green by 3,352.

How this forecast was made

Fitted to those past results, domestic special votes moved towards the Green candidate, against the Labour candidate, by 2017: +8.4; 2020: +5.1; 2023: +7.0 points in an evenly split seat. This year’s shift is forecast at +7.5 points (90%: −4.7 to +19.0). The enrolment change is assumed to have removed voters who leaned to both parties alike, so on average it does not move this contest, but it could go either way (90%: −6.4 to +6.3 points). The official count’s change to the ordinary votes is the one learned from the National–Labour seats. In a backtest that refits the model without each election in turn, its CRPS was 269 votes of final margin, against 226 for the best rule of thumb (seat's swing at the last election); its 90% intervals held 100% of the 9 results.

How the forecast works

The official result is the election-night count, plus the official count’s change to the ordinary votes, plus the special votes. Each part is forecast from the Electoral Commission’s results by voting place for these seats at the 2017, 2020 and 2023 elections (12 seat-elections), and each simulation draws all three.

How many special votes. A seat’s special votes per ordinary vote are stable from one election to the next relative to the country’s: Mt Roskill has had about 25 per 100 ordinary votes, Wairarapa about 13. The model learns each seat’s level and moves it with the national figure. The Commission estimates 399,365 special votes still to count. Its election-night estimates fell short of the final count by 5 to 6% in 2020 and 2023, but this time more may be disallowed from people not enrolled by the new deadline, so the model expects about 412,000, give or take 10%. That is 64% as many per ordinary vote as in 2023.

How the special votes split. Each seat’s special votes are modelled as a shift from its own ordinary votes, on the log-ratio scale against National’s candidate:

\eta_{s,c} = x_s + \mu_c + \alpha + \gamma_c + \beta_s + \varepsilon_{s,c}

where x_s is the seat’s election-night vote, \mu_c the average shift of overseas or domestic special votes, \alpha this election’s national shift, \gamma_c how far overseas and domestic votes part ways in it, \beta_s the seat’s own lasting difference and \varepsilon_{s,c} the rest. Special votes are then drawn from a multinomial distribution with shares \operatorname{softmax}(0, \eta_{s,c}). Fitted to past elections, the national shift of domestic special votes towards Labour’s candidate was 2017: +4.8; 2020: +7.4; 2023: +0.2 points in an evenly split seat. This year’s follows the country’s party-vote lean drawn in the same simulation (next paragraph), plus a seat-level remainder with Student-t tails: +1.7 points (90% interval −11.4 to +14.4).

The party vote. The same idea is applied to the country’s party votes at the 6 elections from 2008 to 2023: for each party, how much its share of overseas and of domestic special votes differed from its share of ordinary votes, relative to National. Each election has a shift common to every party (National’s own good or bad year among special voters, standard deviation about 0.10 on the log-ratio scale) and a shift for each party. This year’s are new draws, applied to the election-night party votes and about 369,000 valid special party votes (90%: 334,000 to 405,000). Overseas votes are a much larger part of them than usual, which matters because overseas votes have favoured the Greens and Opportunity most. The official count has also added about 2.1% to the election-night party votes before any special vote (3 archived counts), leaning slightly away from National; the model repeats that. The close seats’ shift is tied to the party-vote lean with a fitted slope (about 1.0), so a count whose special votes are bad for National is bad for it in the seats too. Seats then follow by the Sainte-Laguë formula with the 5% threshold and overhang, using the simulated winner of 11 electorates and the election-night leader of the rest.

The 2025 enrolment change. The Electoral Amendment Act 2025 closed enrolment 13 days before election day, so nobody could enrol while voting. Those voters used to cast special votes in New Zealand; the national figures imply about 44% of the domestic special votes are gone. They are mostly young and recently moved, so the model assumes, as an editorial judgement, that they leaned away from National compared with the special votes that remain, and towards Labour, the Greens, Te Pāti Māori and Opportunity in the party vote. In the close seats that moves the domestic special votes about 1.6 points towards National (90%: −6.2 to +2.9). The assumption is in config/specials.yml.

The ordinary votes in the official count. The official count rechecks every ordinary vote, and sometimes adds votes that were missing on election night. Election-night counts archived for 8 of the past seat-elections show both: 6 of 8 moved the margin by fewer than 25 votes, but the official count added 8.3% in Hutt South 2023 and 2.4% in Mt Roskill 2023 more ordinary votes than election night. The model gives each seat a 30% chance of such an addition (median 2.6% of its votes), split a little differently from the election-night count, plus recheck noise on every candidate.

All parameters are estimated together with the NUTS sampler in NumPyro, and every simulation draws one set of them, one national shift shared by all the seats, then each seat’s volume, split and recheck.

How well it has worked

Each past election was forecast with the model refitted on the other two, starting from the election-night count (2017, 2023) or from the official ordinary votes where the election-night count was not archived (2020). Three rules of thumb get the same expected number of special votes. CRPS is the average error of the whole forecast distribution, in votes of final margin; lower is better.

Method CRPS (votes) Mean abs. error (votes) 50% coverage 90% coverage Brier (winner) Seats
This model 302 448 0.50 1.00 0.004 12
Specials split like election night 339 431 0.50 0.83 0.000 12
Seat's swing at the last election 396 503 0.50 0.67 0.005 12
Average swing of past seats 376 513 0.08 0.50 0.000 12

The model’s CRPS was 302 votes, lower than 339 for the best rule of thumb (specials split like election night). Its 50% and 90% intervals held 50% and 100% of the 12 results. Its median missed by 448 votes on average, more than specials split like election night (431): a fixed rule is hard to beat in a year whose special votes behave like that rule, and the model keeps a wide range because no one knows in advance which year this is. No seat in the backtest changed hands after election night, so the Brier score rewards the most confident method. With 12 seat-elections the comparison is a sanity check, not proof.

The party vote. Each of the 6 elections from 2008 to 2023 was forecast with the model refitted on the others. The model’s median missed a party’s final share by 0.13 points on average, against 0.31 for assuming specials split like election night and 0.13 for the average lean of past elections. Its 50% and 90% intervals held 51% and 98% of the 45 results. Given the electorates each party won, the model’s most likely seat count was right for 89% of parties, against 57% for the election-night count; 12 of 28 seat counts changed. When a party’s seats did change, the model had given the final number a 51% chance on average.
