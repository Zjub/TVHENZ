\begin{abstract}
This paper studies monetary--fiscal coordination in a forward-looking New
Keynesian policy game. The monetary and fiscal authorities act simultaneously
each period under different, fixed mandates, while a separate loss represents
true social welfare. Private agents understand the stationary policy rules, so
expected future policy affects current demand and inflation. Instrument
adjustment costs make current policy a predetermined state: a policymaker can
alter the position inherited by its future self and, through the rival's
feedback rule, alter future rival policy. This is the strategic-investment
mechanism. We derive a rational-expectations Markov-perfect equilibrium as a
fixed point between the private forecasting rule and coupled policy Riccati
equations. An Australian quarterly calibration compares flexible Nash,
monetary persistence only, fiscal persistence only, bilateral persistence, and
discretionary coordination. Expectations materially change shock transmission
and the policy mix, particularly after a cost-push shock. The narrow inherited-
policy strategic channel is nevertheless small in the baseline: its bounded
contribution is \NKMonInvestmentShare\ for unilateral monetary persistence and
\NKFisInvestmentShare\ for unilateral fiscal persistence. Seven dispersed
initial forecasting rules converge to one stable linear equilibrium.
Forward-looking parameter maps locate economically large policy displacement
only when strong transmission and high adjustment persistence coincide, at the
cost of a thinner stability margin; isolated large strategic shares can instead
reflect cancellation in a small continuation condition. Forward-looking
behaviour is therefore a conduit for strategic interaction, not by itself a
guarantee of endogenous leadership or equilibrium multiplicity.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} monetary--fiscal coordination; strategic
investment; New Keynesian model; Markov-perfect equilibrium; rational
expectations; adjustment costs; Australia.\\

\section{Introduction}

Monetary and fiscal authorities affect the same output gap, inflation rate,
and public balance sheet. Their objectives need not be identical. A central
bank can place relatively high weight on inflation, a fiscal authority can
place relatively high weight on activity, and society can evaluate both using
a third, fixed welfare criterion. These differences create policy conflict,
but conflict alone does not imply that one authority can manipulate the other
or that several equilibria exist. Strategic leadership requires a way for a
current action to change a payoff-relevant future state and a rival response to
that state.

Adjustment costs provide precisely such a state. If changing the policy rate
is costly, today's monetary choice becomes tomorrow's inherited policy
position. A monetary authority that knows the fiscal feedback rule can take
account of the fiscal response to that position. The same logic applies to a
fiscal instrument. With an adjustment cost for only one authority, that
authority has a unilateral commitment capacity. With costs for both, both
authorities invest in inherited positions. The resulting allocation is a
simultaneous Markov-perfect equilibrium, not a mechanically imposed
Stackelberg order. It can become leader-like only when the induced rival
responses are sufficiently strong.

The private economy must also be forward looking. Households and firms care
about expected future activity, inflation, and policy. A promise of future
stabilisation can therefore change current outcomes before the promised action
is taken. This adds a second strategic conduit: current policy changes the
state, the state changes expected future policy, and those expectations feed
back into current demand and price setting. The central question is whether
this rational-expectations feedback strengthens the strategic-investment
mechanism enough to create endogenous leadership or difficult equilibrium
selection.

We answer that question in a compact quarterly New Keynesian model calibrated
to Australian data. Monetary and fiscal instruments enter the dynamic IS
curve, inflation follows a forward-looking Phillips curve, and public debt is
predetermined. Each authority solves an infinite-horizon linear--quadratic
problem under discretion. Private forecasts and policy rules are jointly
determined. The exercise compares five institutional environments:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item flexible feedback Nash, with no adjustment cost;
  \item monetary persistence only;
  \item fiscal persistence only;
  \item bilateral persistence; and
  \item coordination under a separate true social objective with flexible
        instruments.
\end{enumerate}

The distinction between the last case and commitment is essential.
Coordination changes whose objective is minimized; adjustment costs change the
intertemporal technology of policy; rational expectations determine how the
private sector prices future policy. A coordinated discretionary planner is
not a Ramsey planner that can bind all future decisions.

The calibrated results are informative in part because they do not force a
large leadership effect. Policy inertia changes the feedback rules and shock
responses. Forward-looking private behaviour is visible, especially after a
cost-push shock. But the rival's coefficient on the other authority's inherited
instrument is small, so the specifically Petkov-style strategic-investment
component is small. This separates three ideas that are often run together:
mandate conflict, ordinary dynamic spillovers through debt and expectations,
and strategic investment through an inherited policy position.

\section{Connection to the literature}

The private block follows the standard forward-looking New Keynesian logic of
\citet{ClaridaGaliGertler1999}: expected future activity and expected future
inflation affect current choices. Unlike a conventional Taylor-rule exercise,
both policy instruments are endogenous solutions to agency optimization
problems. The model is therefore a dynamic monetary--fiscal game in the
tradition of \citet{OudizSachs1984}, \citet{Tabellini1986}, and
\citet{BeetsmaBovenberg1997}.

The strategic-investment interpretation follows \citet{Petkov2004} and the
dynamic oligopoly analysis of \citet{JunVives2004}. A durable or costly-to-
adjust action changes tomorrow's state and hence a rival's feedback action.
The important object is not adjustment cost in isolation, but the product of
state exposure, the rival's response coefficient, and the shadow value of the
rival action. \citet{CouryPetkov2010} applies related dynamic-incentive logic
to monetary delegation and credibility.

Forward-looking policy games can also possess several self-consistent
discretionary equilibria. \citet{KirsanovaYates2025} show how private
expectations can transmit strategic complementarity across current and future
policymakers when an endogenous persistent state is present. Their result
clarifies what must be tested here: a rational-expectations fixed point can be
multiple, but forward-looking equations do not make it multiple by
construction. The direction of monetary--fiscal interaction can likewise
depend on shock type, transmission, mandates, and policy inertia
\citep{MuscatelliEtAl2004}.

\section{The forward-looking economy}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Private behaviour}

Let $x_t$ be the output gap, $\pi_t$ the inflation gap, $m_t$ the monetary
instrument, and $f_t$ the fiscal instrument. Positive $m_t$ is contractionary
in demand; positive $f_t$ is expansionary. Private behaviour is
\begin{align}
x_t &= \E_t x_{t+1}
       -\sigma\bigl(m_t-\E_t\pi_{t+1}\bigr)
       +\psi f_t+d_t,                                      \label{eq:is}\\
\pi_t &= \beta_p\E_t\pi_{t+1}+\kappa x_t+u_t,              \label{eq:pc}
\end{align}
where $d_t$ is a demand disturbance and $u_t$ a cost-push disturbance. The IS
curve says that current demand rises with expected future activity, a lower
expected real interest rate, and fiscal expansion. The Phillips curve says
that current inflation combines expected future inflation, marginal-cost
pressure represented by $x_t$, and a cost-push shock.

These equations matter strategically because future policy rules enter
$\E_t x_{t+1}$ and $\E_t\pi_{t+1}$. For example, anticipated future monetary
tightening lowers expected future demand; anticipated future fiscal support
can raise it. Current policy therefore has both a direct effect and an
expectations-mediated effect.

The public-debt gap evolves according to
\begin{equation}
b_{t+1}=\rho_b b_t+\chi_m m_t+\chi_f f_t-\chi_\pi\pi_t.
\label{eq:debt}
\end{equation}
This is a parsimonious law of motion rather than a full nominal government
valuation equation. Monetary tightening raises debt-service pressure, fiscal
expansion raises the debt gap, and inflation erodes it.

For the baseline experiment, the innovations are one-period impulses,
$d_{t+1}=\rho_d d_t$ and $u_{t+1}=\rho_u u_t$ with
$\rho_d=\rho_u=0$. Endogenous persistence remains because debt and, where
applicable, lagged instruments are predetermined.

\subsection{Timing and the Markov state}

At the start of period $t$, the authorities and private agents observe
\begin{equation}
s_t=(b_t,m_{t-1},f_{t-1},d_t,u_t)\trans.
\end{equation}
The monetary and fiscal authorities then choose $m_t$ and $f_t$
simultaneously as functions of $s_t$. Private agents observe those choices,
know the stationary policy rules that will be used from $t+1$ onward, and
determine $x_t$ and $\pi_t$ from equations \eqref{eq:is}--\eqref{eq:pc}.
Finally, equation \eqref{eq:debt} and the policy choices determine $s_{t+1}$.

The simultaneous within-period policy move is deliberate. Leadership is not
imposed by allowing one authority to move first. It can emerge only through
the state transition: a current action changes an inherited position to which
future policymakers respond.

The Julia implementation uses a seven-element state vector by retaining two
zero-coefficient bookkeeping locations for lagged output and inflation. In the
forward-looking model they have no economic role; the minimal state is the
five-element vector above. This implementation detail does not add backward-
looking terms to equations \eqref{eq:is} or \eqref{eq:pc}.

\subsection{Agency mandates and true social welfare}

The monetary authority minimizes
\begin{align}
J_M=\E_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta_M^t\bigl[
  &\omega^M_\pi\pi_t^2+\omega^M_xx_t^2+\omega^M_bb_{t+1}^2
  +r_Mm_t^2+\lambda_M(m_t-m_{t-1})^2\bigr],               \label{eq:lm}
\end{align}
and the fiscal authority minimizes
\begin{align}
J_F=\E_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta_F^t\bigl[
  &\omega^F_\pi\pi_t^2+\omega^F_xx_t^2+\omega^F_bb_{t+1}^2
  +r_Ff_t^2+\lambda_F(f_t-f_{t-1})^2\bigr].               \label{eq:lf}
\end{align}
The monetary mandate is relatively inflation-oriented and the fiscal mandate
relatively output-oriented. Each agency bears only the resource or
implementation costs of its own instrument. All weights are primitives held
fixed across regimes.

True social welfare is represented by the separate loss
\begin{align}
J_S=\E_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta_S^t\bigl[
 &\omega^S_\pi\pi_t^2+\omega^S_xx_t^2+\omega^S_bb_{t+1}^2
 +r^S_Mm_t^2+r^S_Ff_t^2 \notag\\
 &+\lambda_M(m_t-m_{t-1})^2
 +\lambda_F(f_t-f_{t-1})^2\bigr].                         \label{eq:ls}
\end{align}
This formulation distinguishes delegated objectives from social welfare.
Cooperation means that a single discretionary policymaker chooses both
instruments to minimize \eqref{eq:ls}; it does not average the two agency
losses or change the primitive weights between experiments.

The adjustment coefficients have two interpretations that should not be
confused. They are real implementation costs in the loss functions, and they
also create a commitment technology because the current instrument becomes a
payoff-relevant state. In the experiments below the coefficients are imposed
institutional primitives, not choices in an outer game.

\section{Recursive rational-expectations equilibrium}
\label{sec:solution}

\subsection{Reducing the private block}

The equilibrium is linear. Let $y_t=(x_t,\pi_t)\trans$ and
$q_t=(s_t\trans,m_t,f_t)\trans$. Before imposing private equilibrium, write
the state transition as
\begin{equation}
s_{t+1}=Nq_t+Ey_t.                                         \label{eq:transition0}
\end{equation}
Debt and the inherited instruments are contained in $N$; current output and
inflation enter the next state through $E$. Suppose private agents conjecture
the stationary forecasting rule
\begin{equation}
\E_t y_{t+1}=G s_{t+1}.                                    \label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:is}--\eqref{eq:pc} can then be collected as
\begin{equation}
Ly_t=Dq_t+HG s_{t+1},
\quad
L=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-\kappa&1\end{bmatrix},
\quad
H=\begin{bmatrix}1&\sigma\\0&\beta_p\end{bmatrix}.       \label{eq:privatecompact}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:transition0} gives
\begin{align}
M(G)y_t&=[D+HGN]q_t,\qquad M(G)=L-HGE,                     \label{eq:M}\\
y_t&=O(G)q_t,\qquad O(G)=M(G)^{-1}[D+HGN],                 \label{eq:O}\\
s_{t+1}&=T(G)q_t,\qquad T(G)=N+EO(G).                      \label{eq:T}
\end{align}
The inverse $M(G)^{-1}$ is the within-period expectations multiplier. It
summarizes how beliefs about the next state feed back into current private
decisions. Partition
\begin{equation}
T(G)=\begin{bmatrix}A(G)&B_M(G)&B_F(G)\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed $G$, the policy problem is now an ordinary linear--quadratic
dynamic game.

\subsection{Coupled policy Bellman equations}

Let the stationary feedback rules be
\begin{equation}
m_t=k_M\trans s_t,\qquad f_t=k_F\trans s_t.                \label{eq:rules}
\end{equation}
For a fixed expectations map, each stage loss can be written $q_t\trans
Q_i(G)q_t$. The matrices $Q_i(G)$ include current output and inflation because
those variables have already been mapped into $q_t$ by $O(G)$. Let
$V_i(s)=s\trans P_i s$. The two simultaneous first-order conditions are
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
Q^M_{mm}+\beta_M B_M\trans P_MB_M &
Q^M_{mf}+\beta_M B_M\trans P_MB_F\\
Q^F_{fm}+\beta_F B_F\trans P_FB_M &
Q^F_{ff}+\beta_F B_F\trans P_FB_F
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}m_t\\f_t\end{bmatrix}
=-
\begin{bmatrix}
Q^M_{ms}+\beta_M B_M\trans P_MA\\
Q^F_{fs}+\beta_F B_F\trans P_FA
\end{bmatrix}s_t.                                        \label{eq:focblock}
\end{equation}
Solving \eqref{eq:focblock} produces $k_M$ and $k_F$. Define
$K=\begin{bmatrix}k_M\trans\\k_F\trans\end{bmatrix}$ as the two-row policy
matrix and $C=\begin{bmatrix}I\\K\end{bmatrix}$ as the map from states into
$(s_t\trans,m_t,f_t)\trans$. The closed-loop law and value updates are
\begin{align}
A_c&=A+B_Mk_M\trans+B_Fk_F\trans,                         \label{eq:Acl}\\
P_i&=C\trans Q_i C+\beta_i A_c\trans P_iA_c,
\qquad i\in\{M,F\}.                                      \label{eq:riccati}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:focblock}--\eqref{eq:riccati} are iterated to solve the
feedback Nash game conditional on $G$. Coordination instead uses the
two-instrument first-order condition and a single Riccati equation associated
with $Q_S(G)$.

\subsection{The expectations fixed point}

The conjecture in \eqref{eq:G} must agree with the outcomes generated by the
policy rules. Partition $O(G)=(O_s,O_m,O_f)$. Rational expectations require
\begin{equation}
G=\Phi(G)\equiv O_s(G)+O_m(G)k_M(G)\trans
                    +O_f(G)k_F(G)\trans.                  \label{eq:refp}
\end{equation}
The numerical algorithm therefore has two loops. The inner loop solves the
coupled policy Riccati equations for a provisional $G$. The outer loop updates
$G$ toward $\Phi(G)$ with damping and repeats until the forecasting residual is
below tolerance. The bilateral baseline takes
\NKBilateralOuterIterations\ outer iterations and has closed-loop spectral
radius \NKBilateralRadius.

\paragraph{Equilibrium definition.}
A stationary linear rational-expectations Markov-perfect Nash equilibrium is a
tuple $(G,k_M,k_F,P_M,P_F)$ such that: (i) each authority's rule is optimal
given the rival's rule and $G$; (ii) the value matrices satisfy
\eqref{eq:riccati}; (iii) private forecasts satisfy \eqref{eq:refp}; and (iv)
the closed-loop transition is stable. This is a local linear equilibrium
concept. It neither presumes open-loop commitment nor imposes a leader.

\subsection{Multiplicity and determinacy}

There are two distinct stability questions. First, a candidate equilibrium
must have a stable economic law of motion, $\rho(A_c)<1$. Second, the nonlinear
mapping $G\mapsto\Phi(G)$ can have one or several fixed points. A fixed point
can be economically stable even if a particular undamped numerical iteration
does not converge to it, so multi-start computation is evidence rather than a
global uniqueness proof.

Local multiplicity becomes more plausible when the derivative
$D\Phi(G)$ has an eigenvalue near or above one. Economically, that can occur
when future policy reacts strongly to the state, the private expectations
multiplier $M(G)^{-1}H$ is large, and the state itself is persistent. Then an
expectation of aggressive future policy changes today's economy enough to
validate that policy response. Weak feedback slopes or heavily penalized
instruments break this loop.

\section{Where strategic investment enters}
\label{sec:investment}

\subsection{Ordinary strategic interaction versus strategic investment}

Both authorities always interact because both instruments affect $x_t$,
$\pi_t$, and $b_{t+1}$. This is ordinary within-period strategic interaction.
Debt adds an ordinary dynamic channel: one authority changes debt and the
other later responds to debt. Forward-looking private behaviour adds an
expectations channel: anticipated future responses affect $x_t$ and $\pi_t$
today.

Strategic investment is narrower. Suppose authority $i$ chooses $a_{it}$ and
the rival's next-period rule is $a_{j,t+1}=k_j\trans s_{t+1}$. A marginal
current action changes the rival according to
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial a_{j,t+1}}{\partial a_{it}}
=k_j\trans B_i.                                           \label{eq:rivalresponse}
\end{equation}
If $a_{it}$ becomes the inherited-policy component of $s_{t+1}$, the
corresponding term in \eqref{eq:rivalresponse} is the rival's coefficient on
that lagged instrument. Current policy is then an investment in a future
strategic position.

The continuation first-order condition can be decomposed schematically as
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\beta_i B_i\trans P_i s_{t+1}}_{
\text{total continuation effect}}
=\underbrace{\mathcal N_i}_{\text{own-state effect}}
 +\underbrace{\beta_i\sum_h B_{ih}k_{jh}\mathcal H^i_{j,t+1}}_{
\text{rival-response effect}},                            \label{eq:wedge}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal H^i_{j,t+1}$ is authority $i$'s shadow loss from a marginal
future rival action. The code reports the rival-response term and separates
states $h$ into macro states and the inherited own-policy state. The latter is
the direct empirical counterpart of the Petkov mechanism.

\subsection{Why an adjustment cost creates commitment capacity}

When $\lambda_M=0$, $m_{t-1}$ does not enter the monetary loss. In the present
model it then has no economic role, and neither authority reacts to it. With
$\lambda_M>0$, tomorrow's monetary authority wants to avoid a large movement
away from $m_t$, so $m_t$ affects $m_{t+1}$. If fiscal policy reacts to
$m_t$ as an inherited state, today's monetary authority internalizes that
fiscal reaction. Thus adjustment costs do not simply smooth a time series;
they change the strategic state space.

A useful scalar approximation illustrates why the effect need not rise
monotonically with $\lambda_i$. Ignoring cross-effects, the coefficient on an
inherited instrument behaves roughly like
\begin{equation}
k_{i,a_{i,-1}}\approx
\frac{\lambda_i}{r_i+\lambda_i+\beta_i B_i\trans P_iB_i}.
\label{eq:persistenceapprox}
\end{equation}
At zero cost there is no inherited-policy channel. A moderate cost makes
policy persistent and can strengthen strategic investment. At a very large
cost, however, the authority can barely move the current instrument, so its
ability to invest in a different future position collapses. The economically
relevant effect is generally hump-shaped rather than ``the larger the cost,
the stronger the leader.''

\subsection{Parameter combinations that make the motive important}

Equation \eqref{eq:wedge} identifies six complementary ingredients:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{State exposure:} $B_i$ must map current policy into a
        persistent state, either directly through the inherited instrument or
        indirectly through debt.
  \item \textit{Rival sensitivity:} $k_j\trans B_i$ must be large. Different
        mandate weights can steepen this response, but need not do so.
  \item \textit{Payoff exposure:} the induced rival action must materially
        affect authority $i$'s inflation, output, debt, or instrument loss.
  \item \textit{Patience and persistence:} high $\beta_i$ and persistent
        endogenous states raise the value of changing the future path.
  \item \textit{Expectations transmission:} a large
        $\lVert M(G)^{-1}H\rVert$ allows anticipated future policies to affect
        current outcomes strongly.
  \item \textit{Feasible movement:} adjustment costs must be large enough to
        make policy inherited but not so large that current investment is
        prohibitively expensive.
\end{enumerate}

These conditions are multiplicative. Strongly divergent objectives with a
weak fiscal multiplier can produce little strategic investment. Strong
private expectations with almost no rival response can change shock
propagation but not create leadership. Conversely, a persistent balance-sheet
state and steep cross-policy rules can create significant strategic effects
even when objective weights differ only modestly.

\subsection{Forward-looking parameter regions}
\label{sec:regions}

To make the preceding conditions quantitative, we solve two parameter-region
experiments using the fully forward-looking model in every cell. There is no
mixing with a backward-looking specification. The first experiment varies the
strength of direct policy transmission and the degree of adjustment-induced
persistence. Specifically, a common transmission scale $\tau$ multiplies both
$\sigma$ and $\psi$, preserving their baseline ratio, while adjustment costs
are represented by the dimensionless share
\begin{equation}
p_i=\frac{\lambda_i}{D_i+\lambda_i},                       \label{eq:pshare}
\end{equation}
where $D_i$ is authority $i$'s one-period own-action curvature in the flexible
Nash equilibrium at that value of $\tau$. Thus $p_i$ measures the fraction of
local policy curvature contributed by adjustment. It is comparable across
transmission calibrations even though the raw $\lambda_i$ is not. The
Australian values correspond to $p_M=\NKBaselinePersistenceM$ and
$p_F=\NKBaselinePersistenceF$.

The strategic statistic in the top panels of Figure
\ref{fig:transmissionregions} is
\begin{equation}
S_i^{\mathrm{inv}}=
\frac{|\text{inherited-policy rival-response term}|}
{|\text{inherited-policy term}|+
 |\text{remainder of the continuation FOC}|}.             \label{eq:invshare}
\end{equation}
It is bounded between zero and one and isolates the adjustment-state component
of equation \eqref{eq:wedge}. The lower-left panel provides an economic-scale
check: it adds the distances between each unilateral-persistence policy pair
and flexible Nash after the demand shock. The lower-right panel reports
$1-\rho(A_c)$. A light region in a share panel is persuasive evidence of an
important strategic motive only if policy displacement is also material and
the stability margin remains acceptable.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{nk_strategic_regions_transmission.png}
\caption{Strategic-investment regions in the fully forward-looking game.
The two upper panels use authority-specific colour scales. Stars show the
Australian persistence share; the common star in the lower panels uses their

\label{fig:transmissionregions}
\end{figure}

All \NKRegionFirstConverged\ of \NKRegionFirstTotal\ cells converge to stable
rational-expectations solutions. Three findings stand out.

First, the Australian calibration lies in a dark region: modest persistence
and baseline transmission produce a small inherited-policy contribution. This
confirms the local decomposition in Figure \ref{fig:channels} using a much
wider parameter neighborhood.

Second, the economically largest displacement occurs when both ingredients
are strong. At $\tau=\NKRegionMaxDisplacementTransmission$ and
$p=\NKRegionMaxDisplacementPersistence$, the sum of unilateral policy
displacements reaches \NKRegionMaxDisplacement. The stability margin falls to
\NKRegionMaxDisplacementMargin, however. Strong policy transmission makes a
current investment consequential, while very high persistence makes it
durable; the same combination moves the equilibrium closer to a fragile
closed-loop boundary. The monetary inherited-policy share reaches
\NKRegionMaxMShare\ percent in this high-transmission, high-persistence corner.

Third, a large \emph{share} need not mean a large aggregate strategic effect.
The fiscal share reaches \NKRegionMaxFShare\ percent at weak transmission
$\tau=\NKRegionMaxFTransmission$ and high persistence
$p=\NKRegionMaxFPersistence$. Yet the associated total policy displacement is
only \NKRegionMaxFCellDisplacement, with stability margin
\NKRegionMaxFCellMargin. In that cell the non-investment continuation terms
nearly cancel, so a modest inherited-policy term occupies a large fraction of
a small denominator. This is why the paper reports shares, policy distances,
and stability together.

\paragraph{Expectations and debt persistence.}
The second experiment holds the Australian adjustment coefficients fixed and
varies the private discount factor $\beta_p$ and debt persistence $\rho_b$.
This separates the strength of the forward-looking Phillips-curve channel from
the durability of the main macroeconomic state. Figure
\ref{fig:expectationregions} reports the largest monetary or fiscal inherited-
policy share, the bilateral policy displacement from flexible Nash, the norm
of the private expectations multiplier $M(G)^{-1}H$, and the stability margin.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{nk_strategic_regions_expectations.png}
\caption{Private expectations, debt persistence, and strategic effects with
the Australian bilateral adjustment costs.}
\label{fig:expectationregions}
\end{figure}

All \NKRegionSecondConverged\ of \NKRegionSecondTotal\ cells converge and the
smallest stability margin is \NKExpectRegionMinMargin. As expected, the private
expectations multiplier increases with $\beta_p$ and reaches
\NKExpectRegionMaxMultiplier\ in the grid. But the strategic-investment share
does not increase monotonically with that multiplier. Its maximum,
\NKExpectRegionMaxShare\ percent, occurs at $\beta_p=\NKExpectRegionMaxBeta$
and $\rho_b=\NKExpectRegionMaxDebt$, where policy displacement is only
\NKExpectRegionPeakShareDisplacement. Conversely, the largest bilateral policy
displacement, \NKExpectRegionMaxDisplacement, occurs at
$\beta_p=\NKExpectRegionMaxDisplacementBeta$ and
$\rho_b=\NKExpectRegionMaxDisplacementDebt$, but its inherited-policy share is
only \NKExpectRegionMaxDisplacementShare\ percent.

This lack of monotonicity is economically meaningful. A larger $\beta_p$
strengthens the passage from expected future inflation to current inflation,
but it can also improve anticipated stabilization and reduce the future shadow
loss that an authority wants to manipulate. Greater $\rho_b$ makes current
policy effects more durable, but can make both authorities react mainly to the
same debt state rather than to one another's inherited instrument. Forward-
lookingness and persistence are therefore enabling conditions, not sufficient
statistics for strategic investment.

\toprule
Parameter & Main channel & When it raises strategic investment \\
\midrule
$\lambda_i$ or $p_i$ & Makes current policy an inherited state & At intermediate-to-high persistence, provided current movement remains feasible and the rival reacts to the inherited instrument \\
$\sigma,\psi$ & Strengthen direct policy transmission to demand & When stronger transmission steepens cross-policy feedback rather than merely improving each authority's own stabilization \\
$\kappa$ & Passes output and policy effects into inflation & When the induced inflation effect is important under the acting authority's mandate and does not destabilize expectations \\
$\beta_p$ & Strengthens expected-inflation feedback & When the larger private multiplier also raises the shadow value of manipulating future rival policy; not mechanically \\
$\rho_b$ and $\chi$ coefficients & Increase durability and policy exposure of debt & When the rival reacts strongly to debt and that response affects the acting authority's loss \\
$\beta_M,\beta_F$ & Raise the weight on continuation payoffs & More directly increases the value of strategic investment, conditional on a nonzero rival response \\
Mandate divergence & Changes reaction slopes and desired policy mix & When it makes $k_j\trans B_i$ steep; disagreement alone is insufficient \\
$r_M,r_F$ & Penalize instrument levels & Lower penalties permit stronger reactions, but can also reduce stability margins \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The robust high-strategy region is consequently an intersection, not a single
threshold: policy must materially affect a persistent state, the rival must
respond to that state, private expectations must transmit the future response,
and the induced action must matter for the first authority's loss. The heat
maps show why changing objective weights or adjustment costs one at a time can
fail to generate the game of interest.

\subsection{Bilateral persistence and the meaning of ``meta-Nash''}

With $\lambda_M>0$ and $\lambda_F>0$, both current instruments become states.
Each authority can attempt to shape its future self and its rival. The
stationary solution is a Nash equilibrium in policy rules with bilateral
commitment capacity. It is not literally a Nash equilibrium in the adjustment-
cost parameters: the authorities do not choose $\lambda_M$ and $\lambda_F$.
A true meta-game would add an institutional stage in which the authorities
choose delegation or implementation technologies, anticipating the policy
equilibrium that follows.

\subsection{How expectations enter on impact}

Figure \ref{fig:accounting} decomposes the two private equations term by term.
Every group of bars sums exactly to the realized impact output or inflation
gap. In the demand experiment, the innovation itself dominates current output.
Expected output and inflation are small because the shock is transitory and
policy rapidly stabilizes the induced state. The figure is useful precisely
because it prevents a visually large policy difference from being misdescribed
as a large expectations channel.

After the cost-push shock, expected future output contributes between roughly
0.03 and 0.05 to current output across regimes. Expected inflation also adds to
current inflation. Policy persistence changes these forecasts, so it changes
current private behaviour even before future policy is implemented.
Coordination produces a different decomposition because its joint policy rule
induces a different forecast map $G$.

\subsection{Feedback rules and the inherited-policy channel}

Figure \ref{fig:rules} displays the solved feedback coefficients. The blank
lagged-output and lagged-inflation columns confirm that the pure forward-looking
model does not use those bookkeeping states. Debt is the dominant persistent
macro state. Introducing monetary adjustment cost gives the monetary rule a
coefficient of about 0.26 on lagged monetary policy. Introducing fiscal
adjustment cost gives the fiscal rule a coefficient of about 0.17 on lagged
fiscal policy. These own coefficients establish policy persistence.

Leadership, however, depends on the \emph{cross} coefficients. Under monetary
persistence the fiscal coefficient on lagged monetary policy is only about
$-0.03$. Under fiscal persistence the monetary coefficient on lagged fiscal
policy is about $-0.01$. Thus current policy substantially binds the
authority's future self but only weakly moves the rival. This is the main
reason the calibrated model does not generate a large leader advantage.

The conclusion is not that forward-looking behaviour is unimportant.
Expectations materially alter the private transmission map and the equilibrium
rules. Rather, the particular mechanism needed for endogenous leadership is
weak because the rival does not react strongly to the inherited instrument.
Expectations amplify whatever future-policy response exists; they cannot
create a large $k_j\trans B_i$ when the policy game produces a small one.

\subsection{Dynamic responses}

Figures \ref{fig:demandirf} and \ref{fig:supplyirf} show the first sixteen
quarters. The one-period innovations disappear immediately, but debt and
lagged policy carry their effects forward. Under an adjustment-cost regime,
the instrument path moves gradually because the current action becomes the
next period's reference point. Private agents anticipate the entire path, so
the impact output and inflation responses already incorporate those future
moves.

The welfare comparison also shows why coordination and commitment capacity
must remain distinct. In the demand experiment bilateral persistence has macro
loss \NKDemandBilateralMacroLoss, slightly below coordinated discretion's

Its actual loss is nevertheless \NKDemandBilateralActualLoss, above the

instruments is included. A restriction can improve macro stabilization while
still being socially costly to operate.

\subsection{Equilibrium search}

The bilateral rational-expectations game was resolved from
\NKSearchAttempts\ dispersed initial forecasting maps, including negative,
zero, and large positive scalings of a reference map. All converged attempts
identified \NKDistinctSolutions\ distinct stable stationary solution. This is
evidence that equilibrium selection is not difficult in the baseline. It is
not a theorem of global uniqueness: nonlinear extensions, a fiscal limit,
capital, persistent shocks, or a stronger private expectations multiplier
could introduce additional branches.

\section{Interpretation and extensions}

\subsection{What the forward-looking formulation changes}

The forward-looking formulation changes the economic question in three ways.
First, an announced future path affects current allocations, so a policy
investment need not wait one period to have macroeconomic consequences.
Second, beliefs about future policy are equilibrium objects. The authorities
optimize taking the private forecasting rule as given, while the forecasting
rule must be validated by their choices. Third, equilibrium multiplicity can
arise from self-confirming expectations even when the policy first-order
conditions are locally well behaved.

It does not follow that forward-looking behaviour always magnifies strategic
investment. If anticipated stabilization makes the future state small, it can
reduce the shadow value of manipulating that state. If the rival's rule is
insensitive to the inherited instrument, expectations mainly transmit the
authority's own future policy rather than a strategic rival response. And if
the closed-loop system approaches instability, a large expectations effect may
signal fragile determinacy rather than useful commitment.

\subsection{Why different objective weights are not enough}

Different mandates determine what the authorities want, but the structural
equations determine what they can achieve by manipulating one another. Suppose
fiscal policy places much more weight on output than monetary policy. This
creates disagreement about the preferred mix of $m_t$ and $f_t$. Yet if
$\psi$ is small, debt responses are weak, and the fiscal rule barely responds
to inherited monetary policy, a monetary investment changes little about
future fiscal behaviour. The disagreement is real but strategically
inoperative.

Conversely, even moderate mandate differences can matter greatly when debt is
persistent, the rival's reaction is steep, and expected future policy strongly
affects present outcomes. The relevant analytical object is therefore a
response product such as
\begin{equation}
\beta_i\times(B_i\text{ state exposure})\times
(k_j\text{ rival slope})\times
(\text{payoff shadow value})\times
(\text{expectations multiplier}).
\end{equation}
Calibration should target these components, not only the relative inflation
and output weights.

\subsection{Relation to fiscal dominance}

The model contains a fiscal-pressure channel. Fiscal policy changes debt, debt
is persistent, inflation changes the real debt burden, and monetary policy
responds to debt. It can therefore study how fiscal actions constrain or
redirect monetary stabilization.

It does not yet fully nest the fiscal theory of the price level or a strong
fiscal-dominance regime. That would require nominal government liabilities, a
consolidated intertemporal government budget constraint, and explicit rules
for monetary versus fiscal backing. The price level would then help satisfy a
nominal valuation equation. Adding that block would be the natural extension
for asking whether adjustment-cost commitment and private expectations alter
the boundary between monetary and fiscal dominance.

\subsection{Most informative next experiments}

The baseline points to targeted extensions rather than an indiscriminate
increase in adjustment costs:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item estimate the cross-policy feedback slopes and the fiscal demand
        multiplier using Australian data;
  \item allow persistent demand and cost-push processes, which raise the value
        of shaping future policy;
  \item add nominal long-term government debt and a government valuation
        equation;
  \item introduce capital or another private endogenous state that can support
        stronger intertemporal complementarity;
  \item solve a Ramsey benchmark to separate coordination under discretion
        from full commitment; and
  \item if ``meta-Nash'' is to be literal, add an outer institutional game in
        which commitment capacities are chosen rather than imposed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This paper formulates the monetary--fiscal problem directly as a forward-
looking New Keynesian recursive game. The authorities choose policy
simultaneously each period, private agents form model-consistent expectations,
and adjustment costs turn current instruments into strategic states. The
solution is a fixed point between private forecasts and coupled policy Bellman
equations.

The framework clarifies the mechanism. Mandate differences create conflict.
Debt and private expectations create dynamic spillovers. Adjustment costs
create inherited policy positions. Endogenous leadership requires all of these
pieces to produce a sufficiently large rival response and payoff effect. In
the Australian baseline, policy becomes persistent and expectations alter
current outcomes, but the rival's response to the inherited instrument is
weak. The strategic-investment motive is consequently present but small, and
a multi-start search finds one stable stationary rational-expectations
equilibrium.

This negative quantitative result is useful. It says that adjustment costs are
a credible route to strategic commitment, but not a guarantee of a leader-like
allocation. The next calibration should discipline the response product
identified by the analytical decomposition and then test whether additional
persistent private or fiscal states create the complementarities needed for
difficult equilibrium coordination.

\section{Numerical algorithm}

For each institutional regime, the Julia solver performs the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item initialize the private forecasting matrix $G$;
  \item construct $M(G)$, $O(G)$, and the reduced state matrices
        $A(G),B_M(G),B_F(G)$;
  \item construct the agency loss matrices $Q_M(G)$ and $Q_F(G)$;
  \item iterate equations \eqref{eq:focblock}--\eqref{eq:riccati} until the
        two feedback rules and value matrices converge;
  \item compute $\Phi(G)$ from equation \eqref{eq:refp};
  \item update $G$ with damping and repeat until
        $\lVert\Phi(G)-G\rVert_\infty$ is below tolerance; and
  \item verify $\rho(A_c)<1$ and the private equations at off-equilibrium test
        states.
\end{enumerate}

The coordinated solution replaces steps 3--4 with one social loss matrix and a
two-instrument Riccati problem. It still requires the same private rational-
expectations fixed point because the planner acts under discretion.
